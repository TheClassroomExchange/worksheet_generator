## Unit overview

A 5-day Grade 3 Algebra unit framed as a parade led by Coco the Conductor and four Canadian animals. Children move beyond Grade 2's three-type sorting into the Grade 3 work of identifying OPERATIONS in patterns (not just additive — also multiplicative and compound), translating across shapes and numbers, justifying predictions for terms far beyond what is shown using the rule, and applying pattern thinking to whole numbers up to 1000. The unit threads a single narrative across all five days, builds a class anchor chart day by day, and culminates in a summative 'Lead the Number Parade to 1000' performance task in which each child invents a rule (potentially compound), builds a number parade up to 1000, organizes it as a table of values, predicts a far term, and justifies the rule formally to a partner.

**Unit learning goal:** Children will identify and describe operations in repeating, growing, and shrinking patterns; translate patterns across shapes, numbers, and tables of values; determine pattern rules including compound and multiplicative rules; justify predictions for terms far beyond what is shown; and use repeating patterns to describe relationships among whole numbers up to 1000.

### Curriculum expectations

- **C1.1** — identify and describe repeating elements and operations in a variety of patterns, including patterns found in real-life contexts
- **C1.2** — create and translate patterns that have repeating elements, movements, or operations using various representations, including shapes, numbers, and tables of values
- **C1.3** — determine pattern rules and use them to extend patterns, make and justify predictions, and identify missing elements in patterns that have repeating elements, movements, or operations
- **C1.4** — create and describe patterns to illustrate relationships among whole numbers up to 1000

### Recurring characters

### Threaded vocabulary

- **pattern** — Something that repeats, grows, or shrinks following a rule we can name.
- **term** — One step or one cell of the pattern. Term 1 is the first one, term 2 is the next, and so on.
- **pattern rule** — The recipe that tells us how to get from one term to the next. It can be a single operation like +5 or a compound recipe like +3 then -1.
- **operation** — A math move — adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing — that changes one term into the next.
- **growing pattern** — A pattern where each term gets bigger by following the rule.
- **shrinking pattern** — A pattern where each term gets smaller by following the rule.
- **table of values** — A two-column chart with TERM and VALUE columns that organizes a pattern so we can find any term quickly.
- **predict** — To use the rule to figure out a term we have not yet drawn or built — even one that is very far away.
- **justify** — To explain WHY our prediction is right — using the rule, the starting value, and the number of jumps. A real Pattern Conductor always justifies.
- **generalize** — To describe how a pattern works no matter which term we pick. Generalizing is what mathematicians do.

### Assessment strategy

- **Diagnostic:** Lesson 1 circle-time observation plus a worksheet check: which children can already identify the operation in a pattern (not just the type) AND distinguish additive from multiplicative growth. Recorded on a class diagnostic checklist.
- **Formative:** Lessons 2, 3, and 4 each have a class formative tracker. A standalone mid-unit formative ('Tables and Operations Check') is administered between Lesson 3 and Lesson 4 as a 12-minute gate before the unit's hardest content (formal justification and far prediction).
- **Summative:** Lesson 5 'Lead the Number Parade to 1000' performance task with a 4-level rubric across C1.1-C1.4. Evidence comes from the worksheet (rule, animal parade, number parade, table of values, far prediction, justification sentence) and the partner-lead conversation. Junior Pattern Conductor certificate awarded at the closing ceremony — Grade 3 version explicitly mentions justification and generalization.

### Pedagogical notes

- Grade 3 children can hold the formal rule notation (+N, -N, ×N, compound) in their heads but most still benefit from oral and movement entry points before written work. Always introduce a new operation orally and with bodies before showing the printed notation.
- Justifying predictions formally is the Grade 3 leap. The justification sentence is: 'I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped ___ times, so term ___ is ___.' Build this frame into every lesson, every conference, every share circle. By Lesson 4 children should be able to produce it without prompting.
- When children invent compound rules, validate and document — these often signal Level 4 thinking. Examples: '×2 then +1' produces 1, 3, 7, 15... (doubles plus one) which is a real Grade 4-5 sequence; '+1, +2, +3, +4' (increasing differences) is technically not a Grade 3 expectation but is a beautiful side-quest.
- Worksheets are end-of-lesson consolidation, not the main activity. Centre activities, partner work, and whole-class share circles are the spine.
- In Lesson 5, do NOT require children to write large numbers from memory — provide a thousand chart or labelled 0-1000 number line so children can copy. The cognitive work is the rule and the justification, not the handwriting.
- Children may use calculators in Lesson 4 for multi-step arithmetic when predicting term 50 with a multiplicative rule (e.g., starting at 2, ×2 each term: term 10 = 1024, far above the children's mental math). The calculator is a tool, not a crutch — they still must explain WHY the rule produces that value.
- Build in opportunities for students to make patterns with their bodies, voices, numbers, AND with shapes (geometric: square, triangle, circle, hexagon as the four 'animals' for an alternative parade) — Grade 3 explicitly extends to shapes.
- Use anecdotal observation notes alongside the rubric. The rubric scores skill; the notes capture how a child justified a prediction or invented a clever compound rule.

### Canadian context notes

- All recurring characters are Canadian animals (bear, beaver, fox, moose).
- Parade can reference familiar Canadian community parades (Santa Claus parade, Caribbean Carnival, local Canada Day parade, Pride parade) in oral examples.
- Avoid US-only references. Use 'colour' not 'color' in printed materials. Use metric units in any number context.
- Number patterns in Lesson 5 use Ontario hundred-chart and thousand-chart conventions; align with what is on the classroom math wall.
- Real-life pattern hunt examples (carried over from G2) can include uniquely Canadian contexts: hockey-game scores by period, Tim Hortons cup-collection sequences, transit map line patterns.
- Rule labels (+N / -N / ×N / compound) are the Ontario curriculum's chosen notation for Grade 3 — kept consistent here to match the teacher's other resources.

## Lesson 1: Welcome Back, Pattern Conductors

| Field | Value |

| Grade | Grade 3 |

| Duration | 50 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.1 |

**Big idea:** Patterns can repeat, grow, or shrink — and the rule that drives them can use addition, subtraction, multiplication, or even a combination.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to identify the operation that drives a pattern and to describe the pattern as repeating, growing, or shrinking.

**Student learning goal:** I can identify the operation in a pattern and describe whether it is repeating, growing, or shrinking.

**Success criteria:**
- I can sort five example parades into the right pattern type and the right operation.
- I can describe the operation between term 1 and term 2 of a growing or shrinking pattern in formal notation (+N, -N, or ×N).
- I can find one real-life pattern in our school and tell my partner the operation that drives it.

**Vocabulary introduced:** operation, generalize
**Vocabulary reinforced:** pattern, term, pattern rule, growing pattern, shrinking pattern
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M3_sound_movement_cards, M6_anchor_chart_words

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Coco the Conductor puppet (already prepped from earlier units, or freshly printed for Grade 3)
- Class anchor chart paper labelled 'Pattern Parade — Grade 3: Operations Drive the Rule'
- Five pre-built example parades for the opening reveal: (1) repeating Bea-Finn-Bea-Finn (rule AB), (2) growing additive 1, 3, 5, 7 (rule +2), (3) growing multiplicative 2, 4, 8, 16 (rule ×2), (4) shrinking 20, 17, 14, 11 (rule -3), (5) compound 1, 3, 7, 15 (rule ×2 then +1 — Level 4 stretch)
- Class diagnostic checklist (provided in assessment_suite stage)
- PATTERN word card from M6 ready to tape on the chart; teacher hand-prints OPERATION, +N RULE, ×N RULE on the chart today
- Real-life pattern photos / examples for the hunt: classroom calendar, hopscotch grid, a hockey scoreboard image, an arithmetic sequence written on the board, a doubling sequence (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32...) like a paper-fold experiment

### Minds On (12 min) — Coco's Five Mystery Parades

**Hook:** Coco is back, parade-leaders. This year he brought FIVE different parades — and one of them is a brand-new kind we have never seen before. Can we name the operation that drives each one?

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up Coco. Say to students: 'Welcome back, parade-leaders. In Grade 2 you sorted patterns into three types — repeating, growing, shrinking — and named rules with +N or -N notation. This year, Coco brings something new: the OPERATION that drives the rule. Watch carefully.'
> 
> Lay parade 1 (repeating Bea-Finn-Bea-Finn). Read with the class. 'Type?' (repeating). Tape the PATTERN word card.
> 
> Lay parade 2 (growing additive 1, 3, 5, 7). 'Type?' (growing). 'Operation?' (+2 — addition). Hand-print OPERATION and ADDITION on the chart.
> 
> Lay parade 3 (growing multiplicative 2, 4, 8, 16). Children will say 'growing' but pause on the operation. Some will guess +2, +4, +8 — the INCREMENTS are growing. A child will spot it: the term DOUBLES. 'Yes — MULTIPLICATION. The rule is ×2.' Hand-print MULTIPLICATION.
> 
> Lay parade 4 (shrinking 20, 17, 14, 11). 'Type? Operation?' (shrinking, -3). Confirm.
> 
> Lay parade 5 (compound 1, 3, 7, 15). 'From 1 to 3, we added 2. From 3 to 7, we added 4. The increments GROW. What is happening?' Pause. 'Coco's secret: the rule has TWO STEPS. Multiply by 2, then add 1. We call this a COMPOUND RULE. Today we just notice it — we will not master it yet.' Hand-print COMPOUND RULE.

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet, facing the five example parades laid in a line.
- Read each parade aloud with the teacher and count or compute the change between terms.
- Predict the type and operation for each parade before the teacher names it.
- Repeat the new words 'operation' and 'compound rule' aloud as a class.
- Volunteer at least one example of where they have seen multiplication used outside of math class (skip-counting at recess, doubling money in a piggy bank, etc.).

### Action (28 min) — The Operation Hunt

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole-group sort and label: lay out four MORE pre-built parades and four label cards (REPEATING, GROWING +N, GROWING ×N, SHRINKING -N). Invite four children up; each picks a parade and places it under the right label, explaining the operation. The class decides whether to upgrade or correct each placement.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Coco needs four parade-sorters. Four parades, four labels. Whoever sorts has to tell us: TYPE and OPERATION. Read the parade out loud first, then point to your label and tell us why.' After each placement, ask: 'Anyone agree? Anyone want to add why?'

**Step 2.** Partner build round 1: each pair gets two sets of Animal Cards (M1) and one Parade Strip (M2). Their job: build ONE growing parade with an additive rule (+2, +3, or +5) AND ONE growing parade with a multiplicative rule (×2 or ×3). They write the rule on a sticky next to each.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'You and your partner build two growing parades. The first uses ADDITION — pick a +N rule. The second uses MULTIPLICATION — pick a ×N rule, but keep your starting number small or you will run out of animals fast. Write the rule on a sticky. When you are done, find another pair and try to read THEIR rules before they tell you.'

**Step 3.** Partner build round 2: switch to a SHRINKING parade. Pairs build one with a -N additive rule. Then the stretch challenge: try a shrinking pattern that DIVIDES (e.g., 32, 16, 8, 4 — rule ÷2). Most pairs will need scaffolding. Validate the attempt; we will not formalize ÷N until later.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now switch to shrinking. First make a -N parade — pick the rule, pick the starting number. Then if you have time, try a parade that gets smaller by HALVING. 32, 16, 8, 4. Each term is half the one before. We do not have a sticky for that yet — Coco is still inventing the notation.'

**Step 4.** Real-life operation hunt (8 min): pairs walk the classroom (and hallway if safe) with a clipboard. Their job: find at least TWO real-life patterns and label each with the OPERATION (not just the type). Examples on the board: calendar dates (rule +1), hockey period scores (could be repeating or growing depending on the game), staircase numbers (rule +1), doubling sequence in a paper-folding demo if available.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now we leave the carpet for 8 minutes. You and your partner find two real-life patterns. Sketch each. Label each with TYPE and OPERATION — not just "growing" but also "+1" or "+2" or whatever the operation is.'

**Step 5.** Whole-class share and chart-build: pairs return; each shares one find. Teacher records each finding on the anchor chart under the correct operation column (ADDITION, SUBTRACTION, MULTIPLICATION). This is the unit's running artifact.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Bring your clipboards back to the carpet. Each pair, share ONE find. I will write it on the chart in the right column. By the end of the unit, this chart will be full.'

**Step 6.** Circulate during partner builds and the hunt. Use the diagnostic checklist to record which children: (a) distinguish operation from type, (b) recognize multiplication as an operation in growing patterns, (c) attempt a multiplicative or compound rule unprompted, (d) justify their finding aloud.

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair during the build phase. Ask one child: 'What is the operation here?' Then: 'How is that different from JUST saying it is a growing pattern?' Note responses on the diagnostic checklist.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Stay with the additive growing rule (+1 or +2) and the additive shrinking rule (-1 or -2). Provide pre-built example parades on the desk for the child to label before building. Provide pre-printed sticky notes (+1, +2, -1, -2) so writing is not the barrier. Pair with a confident peer for the hunt.
- **For extending learners:** After the additive parades, build a multiplicative parade with rule ×3 or ×5. On the hunt, look for a real-life pattern with a multiplicative rule (e.g., a tournament bracket where teams halve each round, or money doubling in a savings example). For the deepest stretch: try the compound rule from the carpet reveal (×2 then +1 — sequence 1, 3, 7, 15, 31...).
- **For ELL / IEP:** Provide a small reference card with three sketches (additive growing 1-2-3-4, multiplicative growing 1-2-4-8, shrinking 4-3-2-1) labelled with the operation in the home language if known. Allow pointing and gestures as valid responses. Provide a sentence frame: 'Type: ____. Operation: ____.' On the hunt, pair with a partner who can scribe.
- **For movement learners:** After each parade, the pair stands and ACTS OUT the operation. Additive: each term, take 2 more steps. Multiplicative: each term, double the number of claps. Shrinking: each term, take a step back. The body action makes the abstract operation feel mechanical.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child distinguish OPERATION from TYPE (e.g., '×2 growing' is more specific than just 'growing')? Can they recognize multiplication as a valid pattern operation? Do they attempt a compound or multiplicative rule on their own? Do they name an operation when describing a real-life pattern, not just a type?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the diagnostic checklist for each child as you circulate.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Distinguishes operation from type, Names additive operation (+N / -N), Names multiplicative operation (×N), Attempts compound rule unprompted, Spots real-life pattern with operation labeled, Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Operation Showcase

**Discussion prompts:**
- What was the most surprising operation you saw or built today? Why was it surprising?
- Did anyone find a real-life pattern that uses MULTIPLICATION or DIVISION as the operation? Tell us where.
- If a parade keeps doubling (×2 each term), how big does it get after 10 terms? After 20? Could it ever fit in our classroom?
- Coco is asking — are operations the same thing as rules? When are they the same, and when are they different?

**Exit routine:** On the way out the door, each child gives the teacher one example using the sentence frame: 'A ___ pattern uses the operation ___.' (e.g., 'A growing pattern can use the operation +5.') Teacher records who can produce the frame confidently versus who needs more practice.

### Assessment (diagnostic)

- **Evidence collected:** Class diagnostic checklist completed during the partner-build phase and the real-life pattern hunt. Captures baseline understanding of C1.1 — including the new Grade 3 marker of distinguishing OPERATION from TYPE — before Lesson 2 introduces compound rules formally.
- **Tracker:** `diagnostic_tracker (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`
- **Rationale:** This is the unit's diagnostic moment. The data tells the teacher who already distinguishes operation from type, who recognizes multiplicative growth, and who is ready for compound rules. Children fluent in additive rules but blank on multiplicative may need a small-group re-loop on multiplication-as-repeated-addition before Lesson 2.

## Lesson 2: Multi-Step Pattern Rules

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 3 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 50 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.3 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.1 |

**Big idea:** A pattern rule can be a single step (+5) or a multi-step recipe (+3 then -1, or ×2 +1) — but as long as it is the SAME recipe each time, the rule still works.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to name multi-step pattern rules and to use them to extend patterns several terms ahead.

**Student learning goal:** I can name a multi-step pattern rule and use it to build the next term.

**Success criteria:**
- I can read a pattern and decide whether the rule is a single operation or a multi-step recipe.
- I can name a multi-step rule using formal notation (e.g., +3 then -1, or ×2 +1).
- I can use a multi-step rule to extend a pattern at least three terms beyond what is shown.

**Vocabulary introduced:** —
**Vocabulary reinforced:** operation, pattern rule, term, growing pattern, shrinking pattern, generalize
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M5_core_loop

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from Lesson 1 (PATTERN, OPERATION, ADDITION, MULTIPLICATION, COMPOUND RULE up)
- Pre-built example parades for the opening reveal: (1) single-step +5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25; (2) compound +3 then -1: 4, 7, 6, 9, 8, 11, 10 (alternating up-down); (3) compound ×2 +1: 1, 3, 7, 15, 31; (4) compound -2 then -3 alternating: 30, 28, 25, 23, 20, 18, 15 — for the stretch group
- Sticky notes pre-prepared with rule labels (+5, +3, -1, ×2, '+3 then -1', '×2 +1') and blanks for invented rules
- Class formative tracker for Lesson 2 (provided in assessment_suite stage)
- Small whiteboards or scrap paper, one per pair, for working out next-term arithmetic
- Calculators (one per pair, optional) — Grade 3 children may use calculators when the rule involves multiplication and the values get large

### Minds On (12 min) — Coco's Recipe Reveal

**Hook:** Yesterday Coco showed us five parades, and one had a SECRET COMPOUND RULE we did not crack. Today we crack it open — and we discover that the rule of a pattern can be a TWO-STEP RECIPE.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Lay parade 1 (single-step: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25) on the floor. Hold up Coco.
> 
> Say to students: 'Read this with me.' (5, 10, 15, 20, 25.) 'Type?' (growing.) 'Operation?' (+5.) 'How many STEPS does the rule have?' (One — just add 5.) 'Right. Single-step. Most patterns we have built use a single-step rule.'
> 
> Now lay parade 2 (compound +3 then -1: 4, 7, 6, 9, 8, 11, 10). 'Read with me.' Read. 'Type? Operation?' Children will be confused. Some will say it goes up, then down, then up. 'Yes! From 4 to 7 we added 3. From 7 to 6 we subtracted 1. From 6 to 9 we added 3 again. The rule is ALTERNATING: +3, then -1, then +3, then -1. This is a COMPOUND RULE. Two steps that REPEAT. Watch how I write it.' Place the +3 then -1 sticky.
> 
> Lay parade 3 (×2 +1: 1, 3, 7, 15, 31). 'Read.' (1, 3, 7, 15, 31.) 'From 1 to 3 we added 2. From 3 to 7 we added 4. The increments grow — that means MULTIPLICATION. The rule is ×2, then +1. 1 × 2 = 2, +1 is 3. 3 × 2 = 6, +1 is 7. Try term 6.' Class works it out: 31 × 2 = 62, +1 = 63. 'Multi-step rules let us build any kind of pattern.'

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet, facing the example parades.
- Read each parade aloud and try to identify the operation between each pair of terms.
- Help name each rule using single-step or multi-step notation.
- Compute the next term for one of the multi-step parades using mental math or paper.
- Repeat the multi-step rule format aloud as a class ('plus three, then minus one' or 'times two, plus one').

### Action (28 min) — Build the Recipe, Test the Rule

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole-group demo of a multi-step build: take a fresh number parade, start at 2, apply the rule '+3 then -1' for 6 terms. Walk through each term aloud: 2 → 5 → 4 → 7 → 6 → 9 → 8. Place a Core Loop Frame (M5) over each PAIR of terms (term 1+2, term 3+4, etc.) to show the rule cycle. Children should see that the loop fits each pair.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Watch this loop. Two-step rules need TWO terms to show the full cycle. From term 1 to term 2, the rule does +3 (the first step). From term 2 to term 3, the rule does -1 (the second step). Then it starts over. The loop frames the cycle.'

**Step 2.** Partner build round 1 — single-step parades: each pair gets Animal Cards (M1), one Parade Strip (M2), and a stack of sticky notes. Their job: build TWO single-step number parades with rules of their choice (one additive, one subtractive). Write the rule on a sticky. Use the parade strip with dry-erase markers to record the numbers.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Quick warm-up. Two single-step parades — one with a +N rule, one with a -N rule. Pick your numbers. Write the rule on a sticky. 4 minutes.'

**Step 3.** Partner build round 2 — multi-step parades: now each pair builds ONE compound number parade. They pick TWO operations to combine (e.g., +5 then -2, or ×2 then +3). They build at least 6 terms applying the rule alternately. They check: does each cycle of two terms apply the recipe correctly?

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now the harder one. Pick TWO operations. Pick a starting number. Build at least 6 terms by alternating the operations. Write the compound rule on a sticky. When you finish, check term by term that you applied the recipe right. Trade your strip with another pair when you are done.'

**Step 4.** Stretch challenge for fast pairs: invent a 3-step compound rule (e.g., +5, then +3, then -2 — cycle of 3) and build at least 9 terms. Trade with another fast pair and have them name the rule from just the numbers.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to extending pairs: 'Three-step compound rule. Three operations, repeating. Build 9 terms — that's 3 full cycles. Then trade with another fast pair. They have to figure out your rule from just the numbers.'

**Step 5.** Whole-class extend-the-rule check: bring children back to the carpet. Show one compound rule on a sticky (e.g., +4 then -2). Without showing terms, ask: 'If the parade STARTS at 10, what are the first 6 terms?' Have the class call out term by term. Then: 'What is term 10? Term 12? Use the rule, not drawing every cell.'

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Stop and come back. Eyes on me. Rule: +4 then -2. Start at 10. Call out the first 6 terms.' Build it together: 10, 14, 12, 16, 14, 18. 'Term 10? Talk to your partner.' (After +4 -2 +4 -2 +4 -2 +4 -2 +4: that's 9 jumps from term 1, so term 10. Net effect of one cycle (+4 -2) = +2. After 4 full cycles (term 9 = 18), one more +4 to term 10 = 22.) 'How did you reason about that?'

**Step 6.** Circulate during partner builds. Use the Lesson 2 formative tracker. Look for: did the child apply the multi-step rule consistently across all terms? Did they self-correct when they accidentally swapped the operation order? Could they extend the parade beyond 6 terms using the rule?

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair. Pick one child. Ask: 'What is your rule?' Then: 'Apply it to term 7 — what is it?' Listen for whether the child SAYS the operation order ('first I do +5, then I do -2'). Record on the tracker.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Stay with single-step rules (+N or -N). Provide a sentence frame card: 'My rule is ___. Term 1 is ___. Term 2 is ___.' Pair with a confident peer who can model multi-step builds verbally.
- **For extending learners:** Use compound rules with multiplication: ×2 then +1, ×3 then -2, ×2 then -3. Stretch: invent a 3-step compound rule. Stretch question during conferences: 'If the rule is ×2 +1 starting at 1, what is term 8? Could you predict term 12 without computing every step?' (Answer: each term = 2 × previous + 1, so the formula 2^n - 1 emerges — celebrate but do not require this.)
- **For ELL / IEP:** Use a sentence frame: 'My rule is ___ then ___.' Allow the child to point to operation symbols (+ - × stickers) instead of writing. Provide a partner-build template with the operations pre-printed and the child only fills the numbers. Use a calculator from the start to remove arithmetic load.
- **For movement learners:** After building, the pair stands and physicalizes the multi-step rule. Step forward 4 steps (the +4), then backward 2 steps (the -2). Each cycle is forward-then-back. Walking the rule helps the alternation feel rhythmic and mechanical.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child distinguish single-step from multi-step rules? Do they apply the multi-step rule correctly across all terms (no operation swaps)? Can they extend a multi-step parade 3 or more terms beyond what is shown? Do they reason about how a cycle of operations changes the value (e.g., +4 -2 = +2 net)?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the Lesson 2 formative tracker.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Builds single-step parade with constant rule, Builds compound (2-step) parade applying recipe, Names rule using multi-step notation, Extends parade 3+ terms using rule, Reasons about cycle net-effect, Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Recipe Showcase

**Discussion prompts:**
- Who built the most surprising multi-step rule today? Tell us the recipe and one term.
- When you used a +N then -M rule, what was the NET effect after one full cycle? How did you figure it out?
- Can a compound rule make a parade SHRINK overall, even though one of its operations is +N? Show an example.
- Coco wants to know — what is the difference between a compound rule like '+3 then -1' and just describing the pattern term by term ('it goes up, then down, then up')?

**Exit routine:** On the way out the door, each child names one multi-step rule using formal notation ('My multi-step rule is ___ then ___') AND gives the first three terms produced by it. Teacher records who can produce both confidently.

### Assessment (formative)

- **Tracker:** `formative_tracker_lesson_02 (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`
- **Rationale:** Multi-step pattern rules are the deepest C1.3 marker for Grade 3 — they require children to hold two operations in working memory and apply them in order. The teacher needs evidence of who can do this consistently before Lesson 3 (tables of values) leans on multi-step rules to populate term-value pairs. Children fluent with single-step rules but blank on multi-step likely need the body-physicalization scaffold from differentiation.

## Lesson 3: Tables of Values for Big Numbers

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 3 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 50 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.2 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.4, C1.3 |

**Big idea:** We can use a table of values to organize ANY pattern — and the bigger the numbers get, the more we need the table because we cannot draw every term.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to translate a pattern across shapes, numbers, and a table of values, and to use the table to predict faraway terms in a pattern that reaches into the hundreds and thousands.

**Student learning goal:** I can build a table of values for a number pattern up to 1000 and use it to predict a faraway term.

**Success criteria:**
- I can take a pattern shown as shapes and translate it into a number sequence and a table of values.
- I can extend a table of values up to term 10 with whole numbers up to 1000.
- I can use the table to predict term 20 (or term 30) without drawing every term.

**Vocabulary introduced:** —
**Vocabulary reinforced:** term, table of values, pattern rule, operation, growing pattern, predict
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M5_core_loop

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from previous lessons (PATTERN, OPERATION, ADDITION, MULTIPLICATION, COMPOUND RULE up — add TABLE OF VALUES today)
- Pre-built example: a +25 growing pattern in three reps — (1) shape parade with 1, 2, 3, 4 hexagons stacked in cells (using simple drawn hexagons), (2) number sequence on a sentence strip 25, 50, 75, 100, (3) chart-paper table of values with TERM column 1-4 and VALUE column 25, 50, 75, 100
- A second pre-built example with bigger numbers: ×10 multiplicative growing — (1) shape pattern with 1, 10, 100, 1000 dots representing each term, (2) number sequence 1, 10, 100, 1000, (3) chart-paper table with TERM 1-4 and VALUE 1, 10, 100, 1000
- Sticky notes for writing rule labels (+25, ×10, multi-step rules)
- Class formative tracker for Lesson 3 (provided in assessment_suite stage)
- Pencil-and-grid paper or small whiteboards (one per pair) for drawing tables of values
- Calculators (one per pair, optional) — used in the partner phase when rules involve multiplication and values exceed 100
- Number line 0-1000 (or thousand chart) visible on the math wall

### Minds On (12 min) — Coco's Three Faces, Big Numbers Edition

**Hook:** Coco brought ONE pattern today and put on THREE costumes — shapes, numbers, and a TABLE. Today the numbers go big. By the end of class, we will predict term 20 of a pattern without drawing 20 cells.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Lay all three representations of the +25 pattern in a horizontal line: shape parade (1, 2, 3, 4 hexagons stacked), number sequence (25, 50, 75, 100), table of values (TERM 1-4, VALUE 25-100).
> 
> Say to students: 'Read the shape parade.' (1, 2, 3, 4 hexagons.) 'And the number sequence.' (25, 50, 75, 100.) 'And the table.' (Term 1 = 25, Term 2 = 50, Term 3 = 75, Term 4 = 100.) 'Are these the SAME pattern?' Children debate. 'Look at the rule. Each term ADDS 25. The shape parade just counts hexagons; the value of each term is hexagons × 25. The table shows it cleanest. Same rule. Different costumes.'
> 
> Now point to the table. 'BIG question. Term 20 — what is the value? Without drawing 20 cells.' Pause. Take guesses. 'Term 1 = 25 = 1 × 25. Term 2 = 50 = 2 × 25. Term 3 = 75 = 3 × 25. So Term 20 = 20 × 25 = 500. We just predicted a parade we never built.'
> 
> Lay the second example (×10: 1, 10, 100, 1000). 'Same trick. Term 4 is 1000. The rule is ×10. We are GENERALIZING — describing how the pattern works no matter which term we pick. Today YOU translate AND predict.'

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet, facing the three parallel representations of the +25 pattern.
- Read each representation aloud and identify the +25 rule in each.
- Watch the teacher walk through the table-of-values shortcut to predict term 20.
- Compute term 20 of the +25 pattern in their head or with a partner before the teacher reveals.
- Repeat the new word 'generalize' aloud as a class and define it together.

### Action (28 min) — Translate Across Three, Predict the Faraway Term

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole-class table-building demo: choose a fresh growing rule WITH the class (e.g., +50 starting at 50). Build the shape representation (50 stacked Beas in cells 1-4, but use TALLY MARKS to keep it manageable), write the number sequence below, then build a table of values together. Headers TERM and VALUE, rows 1-4. Predict term 10 together. Predict term 20 together. The class should see that the table supports prediction without drawing every term.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Pick a starting number — someone shout one!' (Take 50.) 'Pick a +N rule.' (Take +50.) 'Term 1 = 50. Term 2 = 100. Term 3 = 150. Term 4 = 200. Build the table. Now predict term 10.' Class works through 10 × 50 = 500. 'Term 20?' Class: 20 × 50 = 1000. 'Whoa — we hit 1000 at term 20. Coco's Grade 3 number parade can reach our class number-line maximum.'

**Step 2.** Partner translation round 1: each pair gets Animal Cards (M1), one Parade Strip (M2), one Core Loop Frame (M5), and a piece of paper for the table. The teacher assigns each pair a starting growing rule: a +N rule with N between 10 and 100 OR a ×N rule with N = 2 or 3. Their job: translate to all three reps — shape strip, number sequence, table of values — then predict term 10 using the table.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'You and your partner just got a rule. Build all three: shape strip, number sequence, table of values. Then USE the table to predict term 10. Write the prediction at the bottom. When you finish, find another pair and try to read THEIR table.'

**Step 3.** Partner translation round 2: pairs swap rules with another pair. Now they only see a sticky note with the rule and the starting term — NO parade. They must build the table from scratch using only the rule. Predict term 10 AND term 20.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Trade rules with another pair. You only get a sticky now — no parade. Build the table from scratch. Use the rule to fill in TERM and VALUE for terms 1 through 10. Then predict term 20. The other pair will check your work.'

**Step 4.** See the expanded walkthrough below for the keystone reveal moment when pairs realize the table not only ORGANIZES the pattern but also REVEALS the relationship between term number and value. Use the walkthrough script for the whole-class debrief in the last 6 minutes of Action time.

> *Teacher prompt:* Follow the expanded_walkthrough section verbatim. The reveal moment is: 'Look at any +N rule starting at N. Term 1 = N, term 2 = 2N, term 3 = 3N. Term K = K × N. The table is showing us the multiplication table for N.'

**Step 5.** Stretch challenge: pairs build a table of values for a multi-step rule from Lesson 2 (e.g., +5 then -2 starting at 10). Note that the TERM column still increments by 1, but the VALUE column changes by alternating amounts. Predict term 10 by using the cycle net effect (+5 -2 = +3 per cycle of 2 terms).

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to extending pairs: 'Multi-step table challenge. Rule: +5 then -2. Start at 10. Build the table for 10 terms. Notice how VALUE changes — it does NOT go up by the same amount each term. Find term 10 by reasoning about the cycle. Then PREDICT term 20.'

**Step 6.** Circulate. Use the Lesson 3 formative tracker. Look especially for: did the table have correct headers (TERM, VALUE)? Did the values match the rule? Could the child predict term 10 using the table SHORTCUT (multiplication or skip-counting) versus drawing? Did they recognize the term × N pattern in additive growing rules?

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair. Pick one child. Ask: 'Read me term 5 from your table.' Then: 'Term 10?' Then: 'How did you compute term 10? Did you draw every term, or did you use the table?' Record on the tracker.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Stay with simple +N rules (+5 or +10) starting at the same N. Provide a pre-drawn table-of-values template with TERM column pre-filled 1-10 and the VALUE column blank. Children only fill the values. Pair with a confident peer.
- **For extending learners:** Use larger +N rules (+50, +100), multiplicative rules (×3, ×5), or compound rules from Lesson 2. Stretch question: 'Look at the relationship between TERM and VALUE in your table. Can you write a one-sentence rule using BOTH the term number and the value?' (For +25: VALUE = TERM × 25. For +50 starting at 50: VALUE = TERM × 50. Celebrate the formula but do not require it.)
- **For ELL / IEP:** Provide a sentence frame for reading the table: 'Term ___ has value ___.' Provide the table with TERM column pre-numbered AND the first three values filled. Allow calculators from the start so arithmetic is not the barrier. Use a partner who can scribe the partner's prediction.
- **For movement learners:** After translating, the pair stands up and SKIP-COUNTS the number sequence aloud while taking a step on each value. For ×N rules, they CLAP the number of terms (1 clap, 2 claps, 4 claps for ×2) so the doubling feels mechanical. For predicting term 10, they call out 'term 10!' on the 10th step.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child build a correct table of values with TERM and VALUE column headers? Do the values match the rule? Can the child predict term 10 using a shortcut (multiplication, skip-counting) rather than drawing every term? Do they recognize that an additive rule starting at N produces the multiplication table for N? Can they handle multi-step rules in the table?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the Lesson 3 formative tracker.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Translates pattern across all 3 reps, Builds table with correct TERM/VALUE headers, Values in table match the rule, Predicts term 10 using shortcut (not drawing), Recognizes term×N relationship, Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Far-Term Showcase

**Discussion prompts:**
- Who predicted the BIGGEST term value today? What was the rule and what was the term you predicted?
- Look at a +N rule starting at N. What is the relationship between TERM number and VALUE? Can you write it as a one-sentence rule?
- When IS a table of values most useful — small numbers or big numbers? Why?
- Coco wants to know — can we predict term 100 of a +5 rule starting at 5? What is the value? How did you figure it out?

**Exit routine:** Each child whispers to a partner: 'My rule was ___, and using my table I predicted that term ___ has value ___.' Then tidy. Teacher listens for children who use multiplication or skip-counting to predict (Level 3+) versus drawing every cell (Level 2).

- **Tracker:** `formative_tracker_lesson_03 (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`

The teacher needs evidence of who can read and BUILD the table AND use it as a prediction shortcut before Lesson 4 (formal justification of far-term predictions) requires the table as a workspace. ### Expanded walkthrough — The Table Reveals the Multiplication

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* Grade 3 children can read a table of values mechanically (looking up values for given terms) but the deeper insight is that the table REVEALS the relationship between term number and value. For an additive rule starting at N (i.e., +N from 0, or starting at N with rule +N), the value at term K equals K × N. This is the foundation of generalization in Grade 3 and the seed of variable thinking in Grade 4-5. The reveal often does not land unless the teacher walks children through it explicitly. This script captures the moment.

**Setup:** On the floor or whiteboard: a +25 pattern shown in three parallel representations — (1) shape parade with 1, 2, 3, 4 hexagons stacked in cells, (2) number sequence sentence strip 25, 50, 75, 100, (3) table of values with headers TERM and VALUE, rows (1, 25), (2, 50), (3, 75), (4, 100). Beside the three, the +25 sticky. The thousand chart visible on the wall. Coco visible. Anchor chart with TABLE OF VALUES freshly hand-printed.

**Teacher script:**

> Hold up Coco. 'Pattern Conductors, here is the magic of the table. Watch carefully.'
> 
> Point to the value column: 25, 50, 75, 100. 'These look like skip-counting by 25. They are. But look at the relationship between term number and value.' Point to row 1: 'Term 1 has value 25. So value = 1 times 25.' Point to row 2: 'Term 2 has value 50. Value = 2 times 25.' Point to row 3: 'Term 3 has value 75 = 3 times 25.' Point to row 4: 'Term 4 has value 100 = 4 times 25.'
> 
> Pause. 'What is the pattern?' Some children will say '+25.' Others will say 'each value is term times 25.' Validate the second answer. 'YES. The VALUE is the TERM number times 25. The table is showing us the 25-times table.'
> 
> 'Now the BIG question. What is term 20?' Take responses. Some kids will skip-count by 25 twenty times — let them try. Others will say 20 × 25. 'Yes. 20 × 25 = 500. Term 20 = 500. We did not draw 20 cells. We did one multiplication.'
> 
> 'Try this: term 100.' (100 × 25 = 2500.) 'But our number line only goes to 1000. So in this parade, term 40 is the LAST one inside our number line, because 40 × 25 = 1000. After term 40, the values exceed 1000.'
> 
> 'Final stretch. Different rule: +50 starting at 50. What is term 10? Term 20?' (Term 10 = 500, term 20 = 1000.) 'Same trick — value = term times 50.'
> 
> 'This is GENERALIZING. We are describing how the pattern works no matter which term we pick. Real mathematicians do this. So do you, now. Today on your worksheet, EVERY rule will produce a multiplication relationship like this.'

**Expected student responses:**
- Many children will see '+25' first — the additive rule between consecutive terms.
- Some children will skip-count by 25 to find term 20 rather than multiplying. Validate the strategy, then nudge them toward the multiplication shortcut.
- A child may articulate the formula 'value = term × 25' before the teacher names it. Celebrate as Level 4 thinking.
- A child may try to apply the term×N shortcut to a multiplicative rule (e.g., ×2 starting at 1) and get confused — the formula there is value = 2^(term-1), which is a Grade 6+ idea. Redirect by skip-multiplying.
- A child may notice the pattern in the value column 25, 50, 75, 100 IS the multiplication table for 25 — celebrate this connection.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Thinking the +25 rule and the ×25 relationship are the same thing — they describe the same pattern but from different angles (term-to-term vs. term-to-value).
- Applying the term × N shortcut to a multiplicative rule (×N) — gives wrong answers because the value grows exponentially.
- Believing the table only works for small term numbers and 'breaks' at term 20 or 50 — the table extends infinitely; only the values exceed our number line.
- Confusing the term number with the value when reading rows quickly.

**Recovery moves:**
- If a child can't see the term × N relationship: cover the value column. Ask 'what is term 1 times 25?' (25.) 'Term 2 times 25?' (50.) 'Now look at the value column.' (25, 50.) The match is visible.
- If a child applies term × N to a multiplicative rule: build a small ×2 table (1, 2, 4, 8, 16). Ask 'is term 5 = 5 × 2 = 10?' (No, it's 16.) 'Why?' Lead them to see that ×N rules don't follow the term × N formula.
- If a child resists multiplication: have them skip-count by 25 to find term 20 (slow). Then have them compute 20 × 25 (fast). The speed difference sells the multiplication.
- If a child gets stuck on far prediction (term 100 = 2500): point to the thousand chart. 'Where is 2500?' (Off the chart.) 'So term 100 of this rule is OFF our class number line. The pattern keeps going forever, but our display has a ceiling.'

## Lesson 4: Justify and Generalize

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 3 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 50 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.3 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.1 |

**Big idea:** A real Pattern Conductor doesn't just predict — they JUSTIFY. They explain WHY the rule works using the operation and how many times the rule has been applied.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to predict terms far beyond what is shown in a pattern and to justify our predictions formally using the rule, the starting value, and the number of jumps.

**Student learning goal:** I can predict term 50 of a pattern and justify my prediction using the rule and the term number.

**Success criteria:**
- I can predict term 30, term 50, or term 100 of a growing pattern using the rule rather than drawing every term.
- I can produce a full justification sentence: 'I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped ___ times, so term ___ is ___.'
- I can spot when my partner's prediction is wrong and explain WHY using the rule.

**Vocabulary introduced:** —
**Vocabulary reinforced:** term, pattern rule, operation, table of values, predict, justify, generalize
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M4_missing_card, M5_core_loop, M6_anchor_chart_words

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from previous lessons (PATTERN, OPERATION, +N RULE, ×N RULE, COMPOUND RULE, TABLE OF VALUES up — add JUSTIFY and GENERALIZE today)
- Three pre-built broken patterns / far-prediction setups: (1) growing +7 starting at 7 with terms 1-3 visible (7, 14, 21) and gap at term 5, (2) shrinking -8 starting at 80 with terms 1-3 visible (80, 72, 64) and gap at term 7, (3) compound +5 then -1 starting at 10 with terms 1-4 visible (10, 15, 14, 19) and far-prediction prompt for term 20
- Missing Mystery Cards (M4) — 5 per pair
- Core Loop Frames (M5) — 1 per pair
- Number line 0-1000 visible on the math wall, plus thousand chart for reference
- JUSTIFY and GENERALIZE hand-printed on chart paper
- Justification sentence frame card (one large class version, smaller versions for each pair): 'I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped ___ times, so term ___ is ___.'
- Class formative tracker for Lesson 4 (provided in assessment_suite stage)
- Calculators (one per pair) — Grade 3 children may use calculators for the multiplicative arithmetic in this lesson

### Minds On (12 min) — Coco's Far-Term Mystery

**Hook:** Coco wrote three patterns on the board with HUGE gaps — term 5, term 7, term 20. We don't have time to draw every cell. Today we use the rule to JUMP.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Lay the three setup patterns in a line. Hold up Coco.
> 
> Say to students: 'Pattern Conductors, Coco needs help. Three patterns. Each one needs us to find a faraway term — but we cannot draw every cell. We have to JUMP using the rule. Then we have to JUSTIFY — explain WHY our answer is right. That's the Grade 3 move.'
> 
> Point to setup 1 (7, 14, 21, ?, term 5). 'Read the visible terms.' (7, 14, 21.) 'Rule?' (+7, or term × 7.) 'Term 4?' (28.) 'Term 5?' Take responses. (35.) 'How did you know? JUSTIFY.' Listen for: 'Term 5 = 5 × 7 = 35' OR 'I added 7 to term 4 (28).' Validate both. Tape JUSTIFY to the chart.
> 
> Point to setup 2 (80, 72, 64, ?, term 7). 'Read.' (80, 72, 64.) 'Rule?' (-8.) 'Term 7?' Take a moment. 'I started at 80, the rule is -8, I jumped 6 times, so term 7 is 80 - (6 × 8) = 80 - 48 = 32.' Have the class chant the sentence frame. 'I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped ___ times, so term ___ is ___.' Tape GENERALIZE to the chart.
> 
> Point to setup 3 (10, 15, 14, 19, term 20). 'This one is COMPOUND. Rule: +5 then -1. Net effect of one cycle (+5 -1) = +4 every 2 terms. From term 1 to term 20 is 19 jumps, which is 9 full cycles of 2 (18 jumps, ending at term 19, value 10 + 9 × 4 = 46) plus one more jump (+5 because odd-position jumps are +5) → term 20 = 51.' This is hard. We will work through it together in partners.

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet, facing the three pre-built setups.
- Read the visible terms of each pattern aloud and identify the rule.
- Predict the requested far term and try to justify aloud using the sentence frame.
- Repeat the words 'justify' and 'generalize' aloud as a class.
- Watch the teacher chant the justification frame: 'I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped ___ times, so term ___ is ___.'

### Action (28 min) — Detectives at Work — Predict Far, Justify Always

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole-class far-prediction model: take a fresh growing pattern (rule +6 starting at 6). Compute term 10 together via the multiplication shortcut: 10 × 6 = 60. Then chant the justification: 'I started at 6, the rule is +6, I jumped 9 times to reach term 10, so term 10 = 6 + 9 × 6 = 6 + 54 = 60.' Also accept: 'Term 10 = 10 × 6 = 60.' Write both versions on the board.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Two ways to justify the same answer. The first uses the starting value plus jumps. The second uses term × rule. Both are correct. The deeper move is — your justification has to mention the RULE, not just the answer.'

**Step 2.** Partner round 1 — far prediction with single-step rule: each pair gets Animal Cards (M1), one Parade Strip (M2), one Core Loop Frame (M5), and a justification sentence frame card. The teacher assigns each pair a single-step growing rule. Their job: predict term 30 AND justify using the sentence frame. Use a calculator if the multiplication exceeds Grade 3 mental math.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'You and your partner just got a +N or -N rule. Compute term 30. Use the sentence frame to justify. Both partners must say the sentence aloud at least once. Then switch.'

**Step 3.** Partner round 2 — far prediction with multiplicative rule: pairs swap rules with another pair, but now the rule is multiplicative (×2 or ×3) and the starting value is small (1, 2, or 3). Their job: predict term 5, term 7, and term 10. By term 10, values like 3 × 2^9 = 1536 will exceed 1000 — they should notice this and discuss the 1000 ceiling.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Multiplicative rule. Smaller starting number this time. Predict terms 5, 7, AND 10. Use a calculator. When a value exceeds 1000, write OFF THE CHART and tell me about it during share circle.'

**Step 4.** Partner round 3 — backward justification: pairs build a pattern with one VISIBLE term in the middle (e.g., term 5 = 32) and a known rule (×2). They reason BACKWARD to find term 1. Justify using the sentence frame: 'Term 5 = 32, the rule is ×2, I jumped backward 4 times by dividing by 2 four times, so term 1 = 32 ÷ 16 = 2.'

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Backward justification. Given term 5 and the rule, find term 1. The justification frame is the same — but you say "jumped BACKWARD" instead of forward. Try it.'

**Step 5.** Stretch challenge: pairs build a compound multi-step rule (e.g., +5 then -2 starting at 10) and predict term 30 by reasoning about the cycle net-effect (+5 -2 = +3 per cycle of 2). Justification: 'I started at 10, the rule is +5 then -2 (+3 per cycle of 2), term 30 is 15 cycles after term 1, so term 30 = 10 + 14 × 3 = 52.' (Approximate — the exact calculation depends on whether term 1 starts a cycle.)

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to extending pairs: 'Compound rule, term 30. Use the cycle net-effect reasoning we discussed in Lesson 2. Justify using the full frame, including the words "per cycle".'

**Step 6.** Whole-class debrief and chart update: bring children back. Ask 2-3 pairs to share their justifications aloud. Add new GENERALIZATIONS to the anchor chart (e.g., 'For a +N rule starting at N, term K = K × N'). The chart should now have 3-5 generalizations the class has discovered.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Stop and come back to the carpet. Two pairs share a justification. We will write the GENERAL RULE you discovered on the chart. By the end of the unit, our chart will have a list of generalizations Pattern Conductors can use.' Take responses, chart them.

**Step 7.** Circulate during partner work. Use the Lesson 4 formative tracker. Look for: did the child use the multiplication shortcut versus drawing? Did they produce the full justification sentence with rule language? Did they reason backward when asked? Did they handle the ceiling at 1000 with the multiplicative rule?

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair. Pick one child. Ask: 'What is term 30?' Then: 'JUSTIFY. Use the sentence frame.' Listen for the full sentence. Then: 'How did you know it was multiplication, not addition?' Record on the tracker.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Stay with single-step +N rules and small N (+2, +3, +5) starting at small numbers. Predict only term 10 (not term 30). Provide the sentence frame pre-printed and encourage the child to fill the blanks orally before writing. Pair with a confident peer. Use a calculator for any multiplication.
- **For extending learners:** Use multiplicative rules (×2, ×3, ×5) and multi-step compound rules. Predict term 50 AND term 100. Stretch question: 'For your rule, what is the LATEST term that stays under 1000?' (Requires solving an inequality — let them estimate by trial-and-error multiplication.)
- **For ELL / IEP:** Use a sentence frame: 'Start = ___. Rule = ___. Jumps = ___. Term ___ = ___.' Pre-fill the start and rule for the child. Allow gestures and pointing as part of the justification. Provide a calculator from the start. Use a partner who can scribe.
- **For movement learners:** After predicting, the pair PHYSICALIZES the jumps. Each step forward is one jump. They walk from term 1 to term 30 (30 small steps), counting aloud. Then for the multiplicative rule, they DOUBLE their step size each jump (1 step, then 2 steps, then 4 steps...) until they can't take any more steps in the room — which physically demonstrates the 1000 ceiling.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child predict term 30, 50, or 100 using the rule rather than drawing? Do they use the multiplication shortcut for additive rules (term × N for +N starting at N)? Do they produce the full justification sentence with rule language? Can they reason backward to find an earlier term given a later one? Do they articulate the 1000 ceiling for fast-growing multiplicative rules?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the Lesson 4 formative tracker.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Predicts term 30 using rule (not drawing), Predicts term 50 using rule, Uses justification sentence frame independently, Reasons backward to find earlier term, Recognizes 1000 ceiling for multiplicative rules, Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Justify and Generalize Showcase

**Discussion prompts:**

Children who predict correctly but cannot justify are often almost-there — they need the sentence-frame scaffold for one more session before the summative.

## Lesson 5: Lead the Number Parade to 1000

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 3 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 55 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.4 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.1, C1.2, C1.3 |

**Big idea:** Pattern rules also work with numbers up to 1000 — and the same five-phase performance (plan, build, translate, table, lead) we have practiced all week now produces parades for the whole school to read.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to use pattern rules with whole numbers up to 1000, build a number parade, organize it as a table of values, predict a faraway term, and justify the rule formally.

**Student learning goal:** I can build a number parade up to 1000, organize it as a table of values, predict term 50, and justify my rule.

**Success criteria:**
- I can build a repeating, growing, OR shrinking number parade with whole numbers up to 1000.
- I can organize my number parade as a table of values with TERM and VALUE columns.
- I can predict term 50 using the rule and justify formally with the sentence frame.

**Vocabulary introduced:** —
**Vocabulary reinforced:** term, operation, pattern rule, table of values, predict, justify, generalize
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M3_sound_movement_cards, M4_missing_card, M5_core_loop

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from previous lessons (PATTERN, OPERATION, +N RULE, ×N RULE, COMPOUND RULE, TABLE OF VALUES, JUSTIFY, GENERALIZE all up — list of class generalizations from L4 should be on chart)
- Two pre-built example parades for the opening reveal: (1) growing +75 starting at 75 (75, 150, 225, 300, 375 — terms 1-5), (2) growing ×2 starting at 5 (5, 10, 20, 40, 80 — terms 1-5)
- Number line 0-1000 (or thousand chart) visible on the math wall
- Hundred chart 0-100 also visible (some children prefer it for smaller-number rules)
- Dry-erase markers (one per pair) for writing numbers in laminated parade strips
- Calculators (one per pair) — used freely in this lesson
- Summative worksheet 'Lead the Number Parade to 1000' (one per child)
- Summative rubric (one per child OR one class clipboard with student-name rows pre-filled)
- Junior Pattern Conductor certificates printed and ready for the closing ceremony — keep hidden until the ceremony
- Optional: small paper crowns or 'Conductor' name tags for the leading phase

### Minds On (12 min) — From Animals to Numbers up to 1000

**Hook:** All week we have practiced the moves of a Pattern Conductor: identify the operation, name the rule, build the table, predict the faraway term, justify formally. Today YOU put it all together — and Coco's parade goes all the way up to 1000.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up Coco. Beside him, lay the +75 example (75, 150, 225, 300, 375).
> 
> Say to students: 'All week — repeating, growing, shrinking, operations, multi-step rules, tables of values, far prediction, formal justification. Today the LAST piece: bigger numbers. Up to 1000.'
> 
> Point to the +75 parade. 'Read it.' (75, 150, 225, 300, 375.) 'Rule?' (+75.) 'Table relationship?' Some kids will say term × 75. 'Term 10?' (10 × 75 = 750.) 'Term 13?' (13 × 75 = 975.) 'Term 14?' (14 × 75 = 1050 — over 1000!) 'So this rule fits in our number line up to term 13.'
> 
> Lay the second example (×2 starting at 5: 5, 10, 20, 40, 80). 'Read.' (5, 10, 20, 40, 80.) 'Rule?' (×2.) 'Term 6?' (160.) 'Term 7?' (320.) 'Term 8?' (640.) 'Term 9?' (1280 — over 1000.) 'So this rule fits up to term 8.'
> 
> 'Today YOU are the Conductor. Pick a rule — single-step or multi-step. Build the animal parade. Translate to a number parade up to 1000. Organize as a table. Predict term 50. Justify formally. Then LEAD a partner. By end of class, every one of you is a Junior Pattern Conductor — Grade 3 edition.' Tease one certificate.

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet, facing the two example parades.
- Read both number parades and predict terms 6, 8, 10 in each.
- Confirm at which term each rule crosses 1000 (the ceiling).
- Watch the teacher tease the Junior Pattern Conductor certificate.
- Repeat the five summative phases aloud: PLAN, BUILD, TRANSLATE, TABLE, LEAD.

### Action (33 min) — Plan, Build, Translate, Table, Predict-and-Justify, Lead

*Structure:* `individual`

**Step 1.** Phase 1 — PLAN (5 min). Hand out the summative worksheet. Each child works independently. They pick a rule (repeating: AB, ABB, ABCD; growing: +N or ×N; shrinking: -N; OR a multi-step compound rule from Lesson 2). They circle the type, write the rule in My Rule, and draw their core (for repeating) or first 2 terms (for growing/shrinking) in My Core box.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Conductors — first you PLAN. Pick a rule. Write it. Circle the type. Draw your core or first 2 terms. Take 5 minutes.'

**Step 2.** Phase 2 — BUILD an animal parade (6 min). Children draw or sticker their animal parade across an 8-cell strip. For repeating: extend the core. For growing/shrinking: stack more or fewer animals per cell. Loop the FIRST core (repeating) or the JUMP between term 1 and term 2 (growing/shrinking).

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now BUILD. Use your rule to fill all 8 cells. Loop the core OR the first jump. Read your parade quietly to check.' Begin one-on-one rubric conferences during this phase.

**Step 3.** Phase 3 — TRANSLATE to a number parade up to 1000 (6 min). Children pick numbers based on their rule type and starting value, then fill the 8-cell number strip. Calculator allowed. Constraint: every value must be ≤ 1000 AND ≥ 0.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now TRANSLATE. Pick numbers between 1 and 1000. Apply your rule for 8 terms. Use a calculator. Use the thousand chart. Make sure every term stays under 1000.' Continue rubric conferences.

**Step 4.** Phase 4 — TABLE (6 min). Children draw a 2-column table of values with TERM (1-8 pre-filled) and VALUE columns and fill in their 8 number-parade values. Headers must say TERM and VALUE.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now the TABLE. Two columns. Headers TERM and VALUE. Eight rows. Copy your number parade into the value column.' Quick-check each table for correct headers.

**Step 5.** Phase 5 — PREDICT AND JUSTIFY term 50 (6 min). Children compute term 50 of their pattern using the rule shortcut (multiplication for +N rules, repeated multiplication for ×N rules). They write a full justification sentence using the frame: 'I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped 49 times to reach term 50, so term 50 = ___.' If their term 50 exceeds 1000, they write 'OFF THE CHART' and identify the LATEST term that fits under 1000.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now PREDICT term 50. Use the rule, not drawing. Justify formally with the sentence frame. If term 50 is over 1000, write OFF THE CHART and find the last term that fits.' This is the deepest C1.3 + C1.4 evidence.

**Step 6.** Phase 6 — LEAD (6 min). Children pair up on the carpet. Partner A reads their parade aloud (animal first, then numbers, then table, then term-50 prediction, then justification). Partner B has to: (1) name the rule, (2) check the term-50 calculation, (3) say one thing they noticed. Then swap.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now LEAD. Find a partner. Conductor A — read animal, numbers, table, term-50, justification. Conductor B — name the rule, double-check the term-50 math, say one thing you notice. Swap.'

**Step 7.** Circulate during all phases. Use the rubric to score each child against C1.1, C1.2, C1.3, C1.4. By the end of Phase 6 every child should have a complete worksheet AND a partner-lead conversation logged.

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each child during Phase 5 (the deepest evidence). Ask: 'Term 50?' Then: 'Justify.' Listen for the full sentence. Score against the 4-level rubric.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Provide a worksheet with the rule type pre-selected (just repeating, e.g., AB) and a small number bank (5, 10, 25, 50). Pre-print the table headers. Predict only term 10, not term 50. Allow a partner to scribe.
- **For extending learners:** Challenge the child to use a multi-step compound rule from Lesson 2 (e.g., +50 then -10) AND predict term 50. Stretch question during conferences: 'For your rule, write a generalization for ANY term K — using K and your rule.'
- **For ELL / IEP:** Provide a worksheet with the table pre-drawn and 8 rows pre-numbered. Provide the justification sentence frame pre-printed beside the prediction. Allow a calculator from the start. Use a partner who can scribe. Predict term 20, not term 50.
- **For movement learners:** After completing the worksheet, the pair stands and PHYSICALIZES the rule. They walk through the first 8 terms, calling out the value at each step. Then they JUMP to term 50 by computing the multiplication shortcut and walking 50 large steps to a designated 'term 50' spot.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** SUMMATIVE evidence. Look at the worksheet artifact and the partner-lead conversation. For each child: did they pick a valid rule, build it correctly, translate to numbers preserving the rule and staying within 0-1000, organize a correct table of values with headers, predict term 50 using the rule (not drawing), and produce a full justification sentence? Score against the 4-level rubric.
- **How to record:** Score each child on the summative rubric (4 levels × 4 expectations) using the worksheet as primary evidence and the partner-lead conversation as confirming evidence.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Rule chosen, Animal parade correct (C1.1), Number parade correct & within 0-1000 (C1.4), Translation preserves rule (C1.2), Table correct with headers (C1.2), Term 50 prediction correct (C1.3+C1.4), Full justification sentence (C1.3), Notes for narrative

### Consolidation (10 min) — Junior Pattern Conductor Ceremony — Grade 3 Edition

**Discussion prompts:**
- Who would like to LEAD the WHOLE class through their parade? (Take 2-3 children.) They read animal, numbers, table, term-50 prediction, and justification while the class chants the rule.
- Whose rule produced the BIGGEST term-50 value? Who got closest to 1000 without going over?
- What was the trickiest part of the summative? Was it building, translating, tabling, predicting, or justifying?
- What is one GENERALIZATION you discovered this week that you will remember for Grade 4?
- Coco wants to know — are you a Pattern Conductor now? What does that mean to you?

**Exit routine:** Each child receives a Junior Pattern Conductor certificate (the teacher reads it briefly at the front and hands it over with a high five). Each child then leaves the carpet calling out their rule one last time AND the term-50 value as their parade name ('I am Conductor ___, my rule is ___, and at term 50 my parade is ___').

### Assessment (summative)

- **Evidence collected:** Completed 'Lead the Number Parade to 1000' summative worksheet plus partner-lead conversation observations. Scored against the 4-level rubric covering all four expectations (C1.1, C1.2, C1.3, C1.4). Plus end-of-unit reflection sheet completed alongside the certificate ceremony.
- **Tracker:** `summative_rubric (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`
- **Rationale:** This is the unit's summative moment. The seven-phase task (Plan-Build-Translate-Table-Predict/Justify-Lead) is designed so the worksheet artifact gives evidence on C1.1 (pattern type and operation), C1.2 (translation across all reps including table), C1.3 (rule + far prediction + formal justification), and C1.4 (numbers up to 1000). ### Pattern Parade — Grade 3 Summative Rubric

*Used during and after Lesson 5 (Lead the Number Parade to 1000) to score each child against all four Grade 3 expectations on a 4-level scale. Aligned with Ontario Grade 3 conventions: descriptive, observational, growth-oriented. Level 3 is the grade-level expectation.*

| Expectation | Level 1 (Beginning) | Level 2 (Developing) | Level 3 (Achieving — goal) | Level 4 (Exceeding) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **C1.1** — identify and describe repeating elements and operations in a variety of patterns, including patterns found in real-life contexts | With significant adult prompting, identifies the type of pattern (repeating, growing, shrinking) only some of the time. Does not yet distinguish addition from multiplication as the operation. Does not notice patterns in real-life contexts unless told. | Identifies the three pattern types most of the time. Names the additive operation (+N or -N) with prompting. May confuse multiplicative growth with addition (e.g., calls a doubling pattern 'growing fast'). Notices real-life patterns when prompted. | Confidently identifies all three pattern types AND distinguishes additive from multiplicative operations. Names operations using formal notation (+N, -N, ×N). Independently spots real-life patterns and labels them with the correct operation. | Identifies operations in unfamiliar contexts. Recognizes when a pattern uses a compound rule. Articulates the relationship between term position and operation outcome. Spontaneously notices patterns in environments outside the classroom (sports, music, money, recipes). |
| **C1.2** — create and translate patterns that have repeating elements, movements, or operations using various representations, including shapes, numbers, and tables of values | Treats different representations of the same pattern as different patterns. Cannot yet build a table of values with correct TERM and VALUE headers. Does not connect shape, number, and table representations. | With teacher modelling, translates a pattern into a second representation. Builds a table of values with prompts but may forget headers, mismatch term/value, or omit rows. | Independently translates a pattern across shapes, numbers, AND a table of values. Uses correct TERM and VALUE headers. Articulates that the rule is the same in all representations. | Translates patterns into invented representations including formal generalizations (e.g., 'value = term × 25'). Uses the table to predict and reason about relationships between term and value spontaneously. |
| **C1.3** — determine pattern rules and use them to extend patterns, make and justify predictions, and identify missing elements in patterns that have repeating elements, movements, or operations | Cannot consistently name a pattern rule or fill missing terms. Cannot use the rule to predict beyond what is shown. Justifies predictions only as guesses ('I just thought it'). | Names single-step rules (+N or -N) with prompting. Fills missing terms at the end. Predicts term 5 or term 10 by skip-counting (drawing every term). Justifies with surface description rather than the sentence frame. | Independently names single-step AND multi-step rules using formal notation. Fills missing terms anywhere. Predicts term 30 or term 50 using the rule shortcut (multiplication, not drawing). Justifies using the sentence frame ('I started at ___, the rule is ___, I jumped ___ times, so term ___ is ___'). | Predicts term 100 efficiently. Reasons backward from a later term to find an earlier one. Recognizes when a value will exceed 1000 and identifies the ceiling term. Articulates a generalization (e.g., 'value = K × N' for any term K). |
| **C1.4** — create and describe patterns to illustrate relationships among whole numbers up to 1000 | Does not yet build a number parade with whole numbers up to 1000. May write random or counting numbers. Does not connect rule notation to numbers. | With teacher prompting, builds a number parade using small numbers (1-100) and a +N or -N rule. Stays within range but the relationship to the term is unclear. | Independently builds a number parade up to 1000 using a chosen rule. Plans the starting number and the rule so all 8 terms stay within 0-1000. Explains the relationship between term and value (e.g., 'each term goes up by 75 starting at 75 — value = term × 75'). | Uses larger rules (+50, +100, ×2, ×3) and identifies the latest term that stays within 1000. Generalizes the relationship as a formula. Recognizes which rules land EXACTLY on 1000 at some term (factors of 1000). |

### Lead the Number Parade to 1000 — Summative Administration Guide (Grade 3)

*Ties the Lesson 5 worksheet, the lesson plan's six phases (Plan-Build-Translate-Table-Predict/Justify-Lead), and the rubric together. Tells the teacher exactly what evidence to look for during each phase and which expectation each phase's evidence maps to.*

#### Administration steps

1. Before the lesson: photocopy one summative worksheet per child. Print one rubric per child OR maintain a class clipboard with student-name rows. Have the Junior Pattern Conductor certificates printed (Grade 3 edition) and ready for the closing ceremony — do not show before earned. Confirm the thousand chart 0-1000 is visible on the math wall. Provide calculators (one per pair).
2. Phase 1 (Plan, 5 min): Children write rule (single-step or multi-step), circle type, draw core or first 2 terms. C1.3 evidence (rule choice). Walk and check rule-type alignment.
3. Phase 2 (Build animal parade, 6 min): Children fill the 8-cell animal parade. C1.1 evidence (pattern type). Begin one-on-one rubric conferences.
4. Phase 3 (Translate to numbers, 6 min): Children fill the 8-cell number parade with values 0-1000. C1.4 + C1.2 evidence (translation + numbers to 1000). Watch for over/undershoot of the 0-1000 range.
5. Phase 4 (Table, 6 min): Children copy 8 number values into the table with TERM/VALUE columns. C1.2 evidence (table representation). Quick-check headers AND value-row alignment.
6. Phase 5 (Predict term 50 + justify + generalize, 6 min): Children compute term 50 using rule shortcut, write full justification sentence, state generalization. C1.3 + C1.4 capstone.
7. Phase 6 (Lead, 6 min): Children pair up, lead each other through animal/numbers/table/term-50/justification. C1.1-C1.4 confirming evidence.
8. After the lesson: review rubric with worksheet artifact in front of you. For any child below Level 3, note what evidence (or absence) drove that decision. Use for Communication of Learning narrative. Children at L4 across all dimensions are ready for Grade 4 — document for receiving teacher.

**Scoring guidance:** Use the worksheet as primary evidence and the partner-lead conversation as confirming evidence. For each expectation, ask: 'What level of independence and confidence did this child show?' Level 3 is the grade-level goal. Children at L4 on every expectation are ready for Grade 4 pattern work (variables, more formal generalization). Children at L1 on multiple expectations need targeted small-group work in the next math block, especially around multi-step rules and the justification frame.

#### Evidence per expectation

- **C1.1** — Look at Part 1 (type circled) AND Part 2 (animal parade). Did the child circle the right type AND build a parade matching that type AND name the operation correctly? A child who circles GROWING, builds a +N parade, AND can articulate the operation (addition vs multiplication) is at L3 on C1.1.
- **C1.2** — Look at Part 3 (table of values). Does the table have TERM and VALUE headers? Do the values match the number parade in Part 2? A child who fills the table correctly with headers AND can explain the rule across all reps is at L3 on C1.2.
- **C1.3** — Look at Part 1 (My Rule), Part 3 (term 50 prediction + justification + generalization). Did the child write a valid rule? Did they predict term 50 by SKIP-COUNTING or MULTIPLYING (using the rule, not drawing)? Did they produce the full justification sentence? Did they articulate a generalization (formula or pattern)? A child who does all three is at L3, with the generalization being the L4 marker.
- **C1.4** — Look at Part 2 (number parade). Did the child pick numbers within 0-1000? Did all 8 terms stay in range? Did the rule produce 8 valid terms? A child who picks a rule and starting number that produce 8 valid terms within 0-1000 AND articulates the term-value relationship is at L3 on C1.4. A child using larger rules (+100, ×3) AND identifying the ceiling term is at L4.
