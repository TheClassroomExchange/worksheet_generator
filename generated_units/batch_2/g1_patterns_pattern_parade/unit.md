## Unit overview

A 5-day Grade 1 Algebra unit framed as a parade led by Coco the Conductor and four Canadian animals. Children move beyond simple noticing of repetition (Kindergarten) into naming pattern rules, translating one rule into multiple representations, justifying predictions about what comes next or what is missing, and finally applying pattern thinking to whole numbers up to 50. The unit threads a single narrative across all five days, builds a class anchor chart day by day, and culminates in a summative 'Lead the Number Parade' performance task in which each child invents a rule, builds a parade, translates it, and shows the same rule with numbers.

**Unit learning goal:** Children will identify, describe, and create repeating patterns; name the rule of a pattern; translate one pattern across objects, sounds, movements, and pictures; justify predictions and find missing elements; and use repeating patterns to describe relationships among whole numbers up to 50.

### Curriculum expectations

- **C1.1** — identify and describe a variety of patterns involving repeating elements that include numbers, letters, geometric shapes, and physical objects, and use these patterns to predict what comes next
- **C1.2** — create and translate patterns using objects, sounds, movements, and pictures
- **C1.3** — determine pattern rules and use them to extend patterns, make and justify predictions, and identify missing elements in repeating patterns
- **C1.4** — create and describe patterns to illustrate relationships among whole numbers up to 50

### Recurring characters

- **Coco the Conductor** — The parrot leader who keeps the parade in time and asks the rule question. Appears on every lesson cover and every worksheet header. In Grade 1, Coco starts asking children to JUSTIFY their thinking aloud (e.g., 'How do you know? Tell me the rule.').
  *Visual:* Friendly cartoon parrot, red and yellow feathers, holding a small wooden conductor's baton. Big eyes, warm smile. Grade 1-appropriate, soft outlines, no scary features. Visually identical to the Kindergarten Pattern Parade Coco so a child returning from K recognizes him.
- **The Parade Animals** — The same cast that built parades in Kindergarten now wears letter labels (A, B, C) on their vests so children can connect each animal to a rule label. Used to build, fix, translate, and lead pattern parades across all 5 lessons.
  *Visual:* Four characters drawn in matching cartoon style: Bea the Bear (brown, solid vest), Finn the Fox (orange, striped vest), Bibi the Beaver (dark brown, dotted vest), and Moss the Moose (tan, checkered vest). Same outline weight, same eye style, same friendly expression. Each animal's vest pattern fill is permanent (so they are distinguishable in B&W). In Grade 1 introductory examples, the teacher may add a small letter label (A, B, C) on each vest using a sticker to bridge animals to rule labels — this is a temporary scaffold, not a permanent change to the artwork.

### Threaded vocabulary

- **pattern** — Something that repeats in the same order.
- **core** — The little part of the pattern that keeps repeating.
- **pattern rule** — The name we give a pattern using letters, like AB, ABB, or ABC. The rule tells us how the parade goes.
- **predict** — To say what we think will come next, using the rule.
- **justify** — To tell someone why we are sure — to give a reason.
- **extend** — To keep the pattern going forwards or backwards using the rule.
- **missing** — A part of the parade that is not there yet — we use the rule to figure it out.
- **translate** — To show the same pattern rule in a different way — with sounds, movements, or shapes instead of animals.

### Assessment strategy

- **Diagnostic:** Lesson 1 circle-time observation plus a worksheet check: which children can already extend a 2- or 3-element repeating pattern AND describe what is repeating in their own words. Recorded on a class diagnostic checklist.
- **Formative:** Lesson 2 (rule naming), Lesson 3 (translation), and Lesson 4 (justify and find missing) each have a class formative tracker. A standalone mid-unit formative ('The Same Rule Check') is administered between Lesson 3 and Lesson 4 to confirm rule identification and translation have landed before adding justification.
- **Summative:** Lesson 5 'Lead the Number Parade' performance task with a 4-level rubric across C1.1–C1.4. Evidence comes from the worksheet (rule, animal parade, translation, number parade) and the partner-lead conversation. Junior Pattern Conductor certificate awarded at the closing ceremony.

### Pedagogical notes

- Grade 1 children can name and use letter rule labels (AB, ABB, AAB, ABC) but most still benefit from oral and movement entry points before written work. Always introduce a new rule orally and with bodies before showing the printed letter label.
- Justifying predictions ('How do you know?') is the Grade 1 leap from K. Build it into every lesson, every conference, every share circle. Accept partial justifications and feed them back ('You said "because Bea always comes after Finn" — that is the rule. Say it again.').
- Worksheets are end-of-lesson consolidation, not the main activity. Centre activities and partner work are the spine.
- When children name a different valid rule for the same parade (e.g., reading ABBA as 'Bea-Finn-Finn-Bea' versus 'A-B-B-A'), validate both — multiple correct descriptions are a strength, not an error.
- In Lesson 5, do NOT require children to write large numbers — provide a number reference (hundred chart 0–50) so children can copy. The cognitive work is the rule, not the handwriting.
- Build in opportunities for students to make patterns with their bodies, voices, and numbers — not only with paper.
- Use observational anecdotal notes alongside the rubric. The rubric scores skill; the notes capture how a child justified a prediction or invented a clever rule.

### Canadian context notes

- All recurring characters are Canadian animals (bear, beaver, fox, moose).
- Parade can reference familiar Canadian community parades (Santa Claus parade, Caribbean Carnival, local Canada Day parade) in oral examples without prescribing one.
- Avoid US-only references. Use 'colour' not 'color' in printed materials.
- Number patterns in Lesson 5 use the metric / Ontario hundred chart conventions (0-based or 1-based grid acceptable; align with what is on the classroom math wall).
- Rule labels (AB, ABB, AAB, ABC) are the Ontario curriculum's chosen notation for Grade 1 — kept consistent here to match the teacher's other resources.

## Lesson 1: Welcome to the Parade!

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 1 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 45 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.1 |
| Secondary expectations | — |

**Big idea:** A repeating pattern is something that repeats in the same order — and we can use it to predict what comes next.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to identify and describe repeating patterns and to predict what comes next.

**Student learning goal:** I can describe a repeating pattern and predict what comes next.

**Success criteria:**
- I can show or say what part of a pattern repeats.
- I can predict what comes next in the parade and tell my partner what I see.
- I can build my own repeating pattern with at least three different elements.

**Vocabulary introduced:** pattern, predict
**Vocabulary reinforced:** —
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M6_anchor_chart_words

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Printable Coco the Conductor puppet on a popsicle stick (1 for teacher; template included with manipulatives stage)
- Class anchor chart paper labelled 'Pattern Parade — What we know'
- Class diagnostic checklist (provided in assessment_suite stage)
- Small drum or shaker for the clap-stomp warm-up
- PATTERN word card (M6) ready to tape onto the anchor chart at the moment of introduction

### Minds On (10 min) — Coco's Clap-Stomp-Jump Warm-Up

**Hook:** Coco the Conductor is back, and this year he has a parade with more animals, more sounds, and a brand-new question for us: how do you KNOW what comes next?

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet in a circle. Hold up the Coco puppet. Say to students: 'Friends, last year (or in Kindergarten classrooms next door) Coco led parades and we just spotted what came next. This year, Coco wants more from us — he wants us to PREDICT what comes next AND tell him HOW WE KNOW. That is the Grade 1 job.'
> 
> Clap-stomp-jump warm-up: 'Watch and listen.' Demonstrate clap-stomp-jump, clap-stomp-jump, clap-stomp-jump. Pause. 'Eyes on me — what do you think comes next?' Take 3–4 predictions. Most will say 'clap'. Validate. 'How did you know it would be clap?' Listen. The first child who says 'because it goes clap-stomp-jump and then it starts again' has just described a repeating pattern.
> 
> Repeat the sequence once more, then say: 'When something keeps coming back the same way, in the same order, again and again, we call that a PATTERN. Say it with me — pattern.' Tape the PATTERN word card from M6 to the anchor chart. 'And when we say what comes next, we PREDICT.' Mime a small crystal-ball gesture. 'A pattern lets us predict because the order keeps coming back. Today we are going to PREDICT lots of parades.'

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet.
- Watch and copy the clap-stomp-jump sequence; predict what comes next when the teacher pauses.
- Repeat the words 'pattern' and 'predict' aloud as a class.
- Volunteer to explain HOW they knew what came next.

### Action (25 min) — Build the Parade

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole group: lay one Parade Strip (M2) flat in front of the carpet. Place Bea, Finn, Bea, Finn on the first four cells. Leave the fifth cell empty. Ask the class to read the parade aloud with you, then predict cell 5.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Coco's first parade today goes Bea, Finn, Bea, Finn… who do you predict comes next?' Take predictions. Place Bea on cell five. 'Yes — and how did you KNOW it was Bea? Tell your shoulder partner first.' (15 seconds.) 'Eyes back. What did you tell your partner?' Listen for: 'because Bea always comes after Finn' / 'because the parade keeps repeating Bea-Finn'. Validate the language of repetition.

**Step 2.** Show a second example with three animals: Bea, Finn, Bibi, Bea, Finn, Bibi, ?. Then a third with Bea, Bea, Finn, Bea, Bea, Finn, ?. Invite different children to place the next card and explain.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Coco's second parade is bigger. Bea, Finn, Bibi, Bea, Finn, Bibi… who comes next? Tell me, AND tell me why.' (Place card.) 'Now this one is sneaky. Bea, Bea, Finn, Bea, Bea, Finn… who comes next?' (Pause.) 'This one has TWO Beas before each Finn — does that count as a pattern?' Validate that yes, a pattern can have a repeated element. Hint that we will name these in tomorrow's lesson.

**Step 3.** Send children to partner spots. Each pair gets one set of Animal Cards (M1) and one Parade Strip (M2). Their job: build a parade pattern using two OR three different animals, repeat the rule across the strip, and leave the LAST cell empty for their partner to predict and explain.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'You and your partner are parade builders. Pick two or three animals — your choice. Build a parade where the pattern repeats. Leave the LAST cell empty. Then tap your partner and say: "What comes next, and how do you know?" Your partner has to PREDICT and EXPLAIN. Then swap.'

**Step 4.** Circulate. Use the diagnostic checklist to observe which children can extend a pattern, which can describe what repeats in their own words, which can justify a prediction (the new Grade 1 marker), and which can build a pattern with three different elements.

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair. Ask one child: 'Can you read your partner's parade to me?' Then: 'What is the part that keeps repeating?' Then the Grade 1 question: 'And how do you KNOW what comes next?' Note responses on the diagnostic checklist.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Pre-fill the first three cells of the parade strip with a simple AB pattern (Bea-Finn-Bea) and ask the child only to predict cells four and five. Pair with a confident partner. Allow oral prediction without needing to place the card.
- **For extending learners:** Challenge the pair to build a 4-cell core with three different animals (e.g., Bea-Finn-Bibi-Finn). Then ask: 'If your parade had 10 cells, who would be in cell 10? How do you know?' This is foreshadowing tomorrow's rule lesson.
- **For ELL / IEP:** Use the animal name + a vest descriptor together when speaking ('Bea, the bear with the dark vest'). Allow the child to point or place a card instead of speaking. Accept gestures, sounds, or one-word responses as valid justifications. Photo cards of the animals available at the table.
- **For movement learners:** After building on the strip, the pair stands up and walks the parade — one child calls out animals, the other steps in time. The rhythm helps them feel the pattern.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child predict the next element? Can they describe what is repeating, in their own words? Can they JUSTIFY their prediction even partially ('because the bear comes back')? Can they build their own repeating pattern with two or three elements without prompting?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the diagnostic checklist for each child as you circulate.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Predicts next element, Describes what repeats, Justifies prediction (in own words), Creates 2-element pattern (independent), Creates 3-element pattern (independent), Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Parade Share Circle

**Discussion prompts:**
- Who would like to share a parade you and your partner built? Read it out for us.
- What was the part of your parade that kept coming back?
- How did your partner KNOW what came next? What words did they use?
- Did anyone find a pattern somewhere else in our classroom right now? (Floor tiles, calendar, clothing stripes, the alphabet strip on the wall — all count.)

**Exit routine:** As children leave the carpet, each child names one place they will look for a pattern at home tonight AND says how they would predict the next part.

### Assessment (diagnostic)

- **Evidence collected:** Class diagnostic checklist completed during the partner-build phase. Captures baseline understanding of C1.1 — including the new Grade 1 marker of justification — before Lesson 2 introduces 'pattern rule' formally.
- **Tracker:** `diagnostic_tracker (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`
- **Rationale:** This is the unit's diagnostic moment. The data tells the teacher who already extends and justifies a 2- or 3-element repeating pattern unprompted, so they can group flexibly for Lesson 2 onward. Children who only extend without justifying are typical for early Grade 1; children who already justify are ready for ABB / AAB rules in Lesson 2.

## Lesson 2: What's the Rule?

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 1 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 45 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.3 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.1 |

**Big idea:** Every repeating pattern has a rule — once you know the rule (AB, ABB, AAB, ABC), you can keep the parade going forever.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to find the rule of a repeating pattern and use it to extend the parade.

**Student learning goal:** I can find the pattern rule and use it to keep the parade going.

**Success criteria:**
- I can name a pattern rule using letters (like AB, ABB, AAB, or ABC).
- I can match a parade to its rule.
- I can use the rule to extend a parade many cells further.

**Vocabulary introduced:** pattern rule, core
**Vocabulary reinforced:** pattern, predict
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M5_core_loop, M6_anchor_chart_words

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from Lesson 1 — add a hand-printed RULE card to the chart today (CORE word card from M6 also taped on)
- Pre-built parade strips for the opening reveal: one AB (Bea-Finn-Bea-Finn), one ABB (Bea-Finn-Finn repeated), one AAB (Bea-Bea-Finn repeated), one ABC (Bea-Finn-Bibi repeated)
- Sticky notes (small) for writing rule labels next to each loop
- Class formative tracker for Lesson 2 (provided in assessment_suite stage)
- CORE word card (M6) ready to tape onto the anchor chart at the moment of introduction

### Minds On (10 min) — Coco's Mystery Rules

**Hook:** Coco brought four parades today, and each one has a SECRET RULE. Our job is to crack the code.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Lay the four pre-built strips in a row on the floor — AB, ABB, AAB, ABC. Hold up the Coco puppet.
> 
> Say to students: 'Yesterday we predicted what came next. Today Coco says every parade has a RULE that tells us how it goes. Mathematicians use letters to name rules. Watch.' Point to Strip 1 (AB). 'Read with me — Bea, Finn, Bea, Finn. Watch — I am going to label this with letters. Bea is A. Finn is B. So this rule is A-B-A-B. We call it the AB rule.' Write 'AB' on a sticky note and place it next to the strip.
> 
> Point to Strip 2 (ABB). 'Now this one — Bea, Finn, Finn, Bea, Finn, Finn. Bea is A again. Finn is B. So…' Wait. Let a child say A-B-B. 'Yes! This is the ABB rule. The B happens TWO times before A comes back.' Place an 'ABB' sticky.
> 
> Quickly do AAB (Bea-Bea-Finn) and ABC (Bea-Finn-Bibi). Tape the CORE word card onto the anchor chart. Hand-print 'RULE' beside it. 'When we LOOP the part that repeats, that is the CORE. The letters we put on the core — like AB, ABB — that is the RULE.'

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet.
- Read each pre-built parade aloud with the teacher.
- Help label each parade with its letter rule (AB, ABB, AAB, ABC).
- Repeat the words 'core' and 'rule' aloud as a class.

### Action (25 min) — Lasso the Core, Name the Rule

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole group demo: lay a fresh strip with Bea-Finn-Finn-Bea-Finn-Finn-Bea. Place a Core Loop Frame (M5) around cells 1–3. Ask the class: 'What is the rule?' Confirm ABB. Slide the loop along the parade to show the rule repeats — same loop fits cells 4–6. Then ask: 'If I added cell 8, who would be there? Cell 9? Cell 10?' Push the predictions out 3–5 cells beyond the strip. This is the new Grade 1 move: using the rule to extend further than what is shown.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Watch this loop. It goes around three cells — Bea, Finn, Finn. That is the CORE. The RULE is ABB. If I slide the loop forward, does it still fit? Yes! Same rule. So if Coco's parade kept going to cell 8, who would be there?' Take predictions, write them on the anchor chart. 'And HOW do we know? Because of the rule.' Underline RULE on the chart.

**Step 2.** Send children to partner spots. Each pair gets Animal Cards (M1), one Parade Strip (M2), one Core Loop Frame (M5), and a small stack of sticky notes. Their job: build a parade using one of the four rules (AB, ABB, AAB, or ABC), loop the core, and write the rule on a sticky note.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'You and your partner pick a rule from the chart — AB, ABB, AAB, or ABC. Build a parade with that rule. Loop the core with your frame. Then write the rule on a sticky and put it next to your loop. When you are done, find ONE other pair and try to read THEIR rule before they tell you.'

**Step 3.** After the first build, swap pairs. Each pair now reads another pair's parade and writes the rule they think it is on a fresh sticky. Then the original pair confirms or coaches.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Time for parade detectives. Walk to a different pair. Read their parade, find the core, write the rule on a sticky. Show your sticky to the other pair. If you are right — high five. If you are not — they have to coach you. Tell each other HOW you knew.'

**Step 4.** Whole-class extension: bring children back to the carpet. Hold up one of the four rules on a sticky note (e.g., ABB). Without showing animals, ask: 'If I built an ABB parade with Bibi and Moss instead of Bea and Finn, what would it look like?' Build it as the class calls it out. Then: 'And what would cell 9 be?' This previews tomorrow's translation work.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Stop and come back to the carpet. Eyes on me. The rule is ABB. But now A is BIBI and B is MOSS. Talk to your partner — what does cell 1 look like? Cell 2? Cell 3?' Build it together. 'And cell 9? Tell me how you know.'

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Stay with the AB rule only. Pre-loop the first core for them. Give them the AB sticky pre-written. Their job is just to build a longer AB parade and show that the loop slides.
- **For extending learners:** Challenge to invent a NEW rule the teacher has not shown — for example, ABCB or AABB. Then teach it to another pair. Ask: 'Could we have a rule that uses all four animals?' (Yes — ABCD.)
- **For ELL / IEP:** Use letters AND animal names together every time ('A is Bea, B is Finn — so the rule AB means Bea, Finn, Bea, Finn'). Allow the child to point to the rule on the chart instead of writing it. Provide pre-printed rule stickies (AB / ABB / AAB / ABC) so writing is not the barrier.
- **For movement learners:** Have these children CALL OUT the rule rhythmically as they parade-walk: 'A-B-A-B' or 'A-B-B, A-B-B'. The chant helps them feel the rule as a beat.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child identify the core in their own and others' parades? Can they name the correct rule label? Can they use the rule to extend the parade beyond what is shown? Do they justify with rule language ('because the rule is ABB')?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the Lesson 2 formative tracker.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Identifies core (loops correctly), Names rule (AB/ABB/AAB/ABC), Extends parade using rule, Justifies prediction with rule language, Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Rule Showcase

**Discussion prompts:**
- Who would like to share the parade you built and the rule you used?
- Did anyone build a parade with a NEW rule we did not name on the chart? What letters would you give it?
- When you read another pair's parade, how did you figure out the rule? What did you look for first?

**Exit routine:** On the way out the door, each child tells the teacher one rule they built today AND uses a letter chant to say it ('My rule was A-B-B, A-B-B'). Teacher records who can chant rules confidently.

### Assessment (formative)

- **Evidence collected:** Class formative tracker for C1.3 (rule identification and use), plus completed student worksheet 'Crack Coco's Code'.
- **Tracker:** `formative_tracker_lesson_02 (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`
- **Rationale:** Pattern rules are the Grade 1 leap from K. The teacher needs evidence of who has crossed from spotting repetition to NAMING the structure (AB vs ABB vs AAB) before Lesson 3 (translating rules across representations) requires it implicitly.

## Lesson 3: Same Rule, Different Parade

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 1 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 45 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.2 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.3 |

**Big idea:** The same pattern rule can be shown with different things — animals, sounds, movements, shapes, or letters — and it is still the same rule.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to translate one pattern rule into different representations and prove that the rule stays the same.

**Student learning goal:** I can take one rule and show it as a parade of animals, sounds, and movements.

**Success criteria:**
- I can take an animal parade and show its rule as a sound parade.
- I can take a sound parade and show its rule as a body-movement parade.
- I can tell my partner what stayed the same (the rule) and what changed (the things).

**Vocabulary introduced:** translate
**Vocabulary reinforced:** pattern, core, pattern rule
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M3_sound_movement_cards, M5_core_loop

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from previous lessons (PATTERN, CORE, RULE already up)
- Three pre-built parade strips for the opening reveal: one animal ABB (Bea-Finn-Finn), one sound ABB (clap-stomp-stomp using M3 cards), one body ABB (tall-small-small with photo cards or improvised)
- Sound and Movement Cards (M3) — full set on the carpet, plus 5 sets for partner translation
- Sticky notes pre-written with rule labels (AB, ABB, AAB, ABC) for the assignment phase
- Class formative tracker for Lesson 3 (provided in assessment_suite stage)

### Minds On (10 min) — Coco's Three Parade Mystery

**Hook:** Coco built three parades that look totally different — but he says they all follow the SAME rule. Are we sure?

**Teacher script:**

### Action (25 min) — Translate the Rule

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole group: see the expanded walkthrough below. The opening reveal of the three same-rule parades is fully scripted there. After the reveal, leave all three strips, their loops, and the rule sticky (ABB) in view as a reference for partner work.

> *Teacher prompt:* Follow the expanded_walkthrough section verbatim. Leave the three strips and the rule sticky in view as anchors during partner work.

**Step 2.** Hand each pair a pre-built animal strip showing one rule (assign by readiness — AB, ABB, AAB, or ABC). Their job is to translate the same rule into TWO new versions: a sound version using Sound and Movement Cards (M3), and a body-movement version (no cards — they invent it). They write the rule on a sticky and place it next to all three.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'You and your partner just got an animal parade. Find its rule and write it on a sticky. Then translate the SAME rule into TWO more parades — one with sound cards, one with your bodies. The rule has to stay the same. Only the things change. When you finish, tap your partner and say: "Same rule?" and show all three.'

**Step 3.** Circulate. Each pair must show all three versions before they finish. Ask each child individually: 'What stayed the same in all three?' (the rule) and 'What changed?' (the things). > *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair. Ask one child to read all three versions: animal, sound, body. Then: 'What stayed the same?' Listen for: 'the rule' / 'ABB' / 'the letters'. Then: 'What changed?' Listen for: 'the animals' / 'the sounds' / 'the bodies'. Record on the tracker.

**Step 4.** Optional extension for early-finishing pairs: swap the rule sticky with another pair. Now they have a new rule to translate into three forms — animal, sound, body. This forces them to BUILD from the rule rather than READ from a parade.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to extending pairs: 'Trade rules with another pair. Now you only have a sticky — you do not have a parade. Build all THREE parades from just the rule. Animal, sound, body. Same rule.'

#### Differentiation

Provide pre-printed letter rule cards (A, B, C) so the child can place letters above each cell to bridge.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child translate the rule into a second and third version? Can they articulate that the rule stayed the same and only the surface changed? ### Expanded walkthrough — The Three Parades Reveal — Translating the Rule

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* Grade 1 children naturally read patterns by surface features (the animals, the colours, the sound). C1.2 requires them to abstract past the surface to the underlying RULE. This is the unit's conceptual hinge, and the moment is fragile — many children will say 'no, they are different' the first time. This script walks the teacher through the reveal step by step, including how to recover when the abstraction does not land. The Grade 1 difference from Kindergarten: in K we just named what stayed the same as 'the core'; in Grade 1 we name it as 'the RULE' using letter labels (AB, ABB, etc.). Insist on the rule label.

**Setup:** Three short parade strips, all sharing the SAME ABB rule, laid side by side on the carpet: (1) Bea-Finn-Finn-Bea-Finn-Finn animal version, (2) clap-stomp-stomp-clap-stomp-stomp version using Sound and Movement Cards, (3) tall-small-small-tall-small-small using body-position photo cards or improvised body shapes. A Core Loop Frame ready in the teacher's hand. A blank sticky note ready to write the rule label. Coco the puppet visible. Anchor chart with PATTERN, CORE, RULE already taped on.

**Teacher script:**

> Hold up Coco. Say to students: 'Coco showed me three parades today, and Coco swears they have the SAME rule. Let's see if we can prove it.'
> 
> Point to Strip 1 (animals). 'Read this parade with me.' Lead the class: 'Bea, Finn, Finn, Bea, Finn, Finn.' Place the loop around the first Bea-Finn-Finn group. 'And what is the core here?' (Bea-Finn-Finn.) 'And the rule?' Wait. Let a child say A-B-B. Write 'ABB' on a sticky and place it next to the strip. 'Yes — ABB.'
> 
> Point to Strip 2 (clap-stomp-stomp). 'Now this parade — read it with your hands and feet.' Lead the class: clap, stomp, stomp, clap, stomp, stomp. Pick up the loop and place it around the first clap-stomp-stomp group. 'What is the core?' (clap-stomp-stomp.) 'And the rule?' Let a child say A-B-B. 'Same rule!' Write another 'ABB' sticky and place it next to Strip 2.
> 
> Point to Strip 3 (tall-small-small). 'And this one — let's do it with our bodies. Stand TALL when I point here, crouch SMALL when I point there.' Lead the class through: tall, small, small, tall, small, small. Loop the first tall-small-small group. 'What is the core?' (tall-small-small.) 'And the rule?' (ABB.) Place the third 'ABB' sticky.
> 
> Pause. Step back. Show the three identical 'ABB' stickies in a row. 'Coco said all three parades had the same rule. Look — three stickies, all say ABB. The RULE is the same. Only the THINGS we used are different. Animals, sounds, bodies — but all ABB.'
> 
> Pause and let it land. 'When the rule is the same, the pattern is the SAME pattern. That is what TRANSLATE means — same rule, different things. Coco wins. No cookies for us.'
> 
> Then the Grade 1 stretch: 'Quick — partner talk. If I made a FOURTH parade with the same rule but used shapes instead — circle, square, square, circle, square, square — would that still be ABB?' (Yes.) 'And if I used numbers — 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2 — same rule?' (Yes.) 'The rule does not care what we use. The rule is the rule.'

**Expected student responses:**
- Several children will insist the parades are different because the things in them are different — this is normal and expected.
- Some children will count cells ('they all have six') without seeing ABB structure.
- A child may say 'they all go ONE-TWO-TWO, ONE-TWO-TWO' before the teacher names it ABB — validate this and supply the formal label.
- A child may immediately recognize ABB and say 'they are all ABB' — invite them to teach the class.
- A child may confuse ABB with AAB on the sound version (clap-clap-stomp vs clap-stomp-stomp) — common slip; loop the core slowly to clarify.

**Common misconceptions:**
- 'They are different patterns because one has bears and the other has claps.' — surface-feature thinking, the most common Grade 1 response on first exposure.
- 'It's the same because they have the same number of things.' — confusion between length and rule.
- 'It's ABB because there are three things.' — confusion between length of the core and its structure (any 3-cell core could be ABB, AAB, or ABC).
- Saying 'AB' for an ABB parade — child read the first two cells only and stopped.

**Recovery moves:**
- If a child is stuck on surface features: physically slide all three loops on top of each other and ask 'do the loops have the same letters? Same A and same B in the same order?'
- If a child confuses length and rule: lay out an 8-cell animal parade and a 4-cell clap-stomp parade with the same AB core. Ask 'is the rule the same?' Lead them to see length is independent of rule.
- If a child says ABB for any 3-cell pattern: take an AAB pattern (Bea-Bea-Finn) and ask 'is this ABB?' Walk through pointing letter by letter: 'A-A-B. Two As, then a B. Different rule.'
- If a child names the structure in their own words: validate first, then supply the formal language. 'Yes — you said ONE-TWO-TWO, ONE-TWO-TWO. Mathematicians call that an ABB rule. Same idea, just letters.'

## Lesson 4: Detective Parade

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Grade 1 |
| Strand | C. Algebra (Patterns and Relationships) |
| Duration | 45 minutes |
| Primary expectations | C1.3 |
| Secondary expectations | C1.1 |

**Big idea:** We can use the rule to find missing animals anywhere in the parade and to explain — out loud — why our prediction is right.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to find missing elements in repeating patterns and to justify our predictions using the rule.

**Student learning goal:** I can find a missing animal and tell my partner why I am sure.

**Success criteria:**
- I can find a missing animal at the start, middle, OR end of a parade.
- I can use the rule to explain why my answer is right.
- I can extend a parade forward AND backward using the rule.

**Vocabulary introduced:** justify
**Vocabulary reinforced:** pattern, core, pattern rule, missing, extend, predict
**Manipulatives used:** M1_animal_cards, M2_parade_strip, M4_missing_card, M5_core_loop, M6_anchor_chart_words

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart from previous lessons (PATTERN, CORE, RULE up; add MISSING and EXTEND from M6 today; hand-print JUSTIFY beside them)
- Three pre-built broken parade strips for the opening reveal: one with a missing middle (Bea-Finn-?-Bea-Finn), one with a missing end (Bea-Finn-Finn-Bea-Finn-?), one with a missing START (?-Finn-Bea-Finn-Bea-Finn)
- Missing Mystery Cards (M4) — 5 per pair
- Core Loop Frames (M5) — 1 per pair
- MISSING and EXTEND word cards (M6) ready to tape on the chart
- Class formative tracker for Lesson 4 (provided in assessment_suite stage)

### Minds On (10 min) — Coco's Broken Parades

**Hook:** Coco needs detectives! Three of his parades have animals MISSING — at the start, the middle, or the end. Can we crack the case?

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Lay the three broken strips in a row, each with a Missing Mystery Card (M4) in the gap.
> 
> Say to students: 'Friends, big problem. Coco's parades have GAPS. One has a missing animal in the MIDDLE, one at the END, one at the START. We are detectives today. Our job is to figure out who is missing AND, the Grade 1 rule, JUSTIFY — that means TELL me HOW we know.' Tape the JUSTIFY card hand-printed on chart paper. 'Justify means: give a reason. We are not just guessing. We use the RULE.'
> 
> Point to Strip 1 (missing middle): Bea-Finn-?-Bea-Finn. 'Look. Whose card is in the gap?' Take predictions. Push: 'How do you KNOW?' Listen for: 'because the rule is AB, and after Bea comes Finn'. Validate.
> 
> Quickly walk through Strip 2 (missing end) and Strip 3 (missing start). The missing-start case is the hardest — children must extend BACKWARD using the rule. Reassure that backward thinking is detective thinking.

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet.
- Predict the missing animal in each broken parade.
- Practice using the sentence frame: 'I think it is ___ because the rule is ___.'
- Repeat the new word 'justify' aloud as a class.

### Action (25 min) — Detectives at Work — Fix and Extend

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole group demo: take a clean strip and build Bea-Finn-Finn-Bea-Finn-Finn-?-? leaving the last two cells empty. Say: 'Two animals missing at the end. Find the rule first, then fill the gaps.' Take predictions and justifications. Then: 'Now I am adding ?-?-Bea-Finn-Finn at the START. Find what came BEFORE.' Walk children through using the rule to extend backward. Emphasize: same rule, just running it backward.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Detectives — first the rule, then the gap. Always. The rule is your magnifying glass.' Model the sentence: 'The rule is ABB, so cell 7 is Bea, and cell 8 is Finn, because after Finn-Finn the rule starts over with Bea.'

**Step 2.** Send children to partner spots. Each pair gets Animal Cards (M1), one Parade Strip (M2), 5 Missing Mystery Cards (M4), and one Core Loop Frame (M5). The first child builds a broken parade (must include at least one Missing Card somewhere — start, middle, or end). The second child plays detective: identifies the rule, fills the gap, and justifies aloud. Then they swap.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Partner A: build a broken parade. You pick the rule. Hide one or two animals using Missing Cards. Partner B: find the rule first, then fill the gap, then JUSTIFY out loud. Swap. The detective who can SAY HOW THEY KNOW gets a high five from Coco.'

**Step 3.** Stretch challenge: pairs exchange parades with another pair. Each pair now has a stranger's broken parade. They must extend it BOTH directions — add 2 cells at the start AND 2 cells at the end — using the rule. This combines find-the-missing with extend-both-ways.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Time to swap with another pair. Now the parade is from a stranger! Find the rule, fix any gaps, and add TWO cells at the front and TWO cells at the back. Show your work to the pair who built it. They have to agree the rule is right.'

**Step 4.** Circulate. Use the formative tracker. Look especially for: did the child JUSTIFY out loud, not just place a card silently? Could they extend backward, not just forward? The tracker columns capture position-of-gap difficulty (start vs middle vs end) because that order reflects difficulty.

> *Teacher prompt:* Stop at each pair. Pick one child. Ask: 'How did you know what was missing here?' Then: 'And what would come BEFORE the start of the parade? How do you know?' Record on the tracker.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Stay with AB rules and middle-gaps only. Provide a sentence frame card: 'I think it is ___ because the rule is ___.' Pair with a confident peer who can model the justification first. Accept gestures or pointing to the rule sticker as a valid justification.
- **For extending learners:** Use ABB, AAB, or ABC rules. Multiple gaps in one parade. Add the requirement: extend the parade 4 cells in both directions. For super-extending pairs, ask: 'If this parade had 20 cells, who would be in cell 20? Use the rule to figure it out without drawing every cell.'
- **For ELL / IEP:** Use a sentence frame card: 'It is ___. Rule is ___.' Allow the child to point to the rule label on the chart while saying the animal name. Accept any verbal justification, however short. Provide a parade with only ONE missing cell to reduce load.
- **For movement learners:** After fixing the strip, the pair walks the FULL repaired parade including the extensions, calling out the rule rhythmically. Walking the extension backward (taking 4 steps backward while calling A-B-B in reverse) helps the backward-extension idea stick.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Can the child find a missing element in different positions (start hardest, end easiest)? Can they justify using rule language ('because the rule is ABB')? Can they extend forward AND backward? Do they pick up the rule from a stranger's parade independently?
- **How to record:** Tick the appropriate column on the Lesson 4 formative tracker.
- **Tracker columns:** Student name, Finds missing in middle, Finds missing at end, Finds missing at start, Extends forward using rule, Extends backward using rule, Justifies with rule language, Notes

### Consolidation (10 min) — Detective Share Circle

**Discussion prompts:**
- Who solved a tricky parade today? Tell us the rule and how you knew what was missing.
- Was it harder to find the missing animal at the START or at the END? Why do you think that is?
- If your parade had 100 cells, could you tell me who would be in cell 100? How would you figure it out?

**Exit routine:** On the way out the door, each child says one full justify sentence: 'I am a Pattern Detective because I can use the rule ___ to find a missing ___.' Teacher nods or quietly redirects.

### Assessment (formative)

- **Evidence collected:** Class formative tracker for C1.3 (find missing + justify), plus completed student worksheet 'Pattern Detective'.
- **Tracker:** `formative_tracker_lesson_04 (assembled in assessment_suite stage)`

The teacher needs evidence of who can verbalize the rule-as-reason before Lesson 5 (the summative) requires it formally. The position-of-gap columns reflect known difficulty: middle and end gaps are within reach by Grade 1; start gaps and backward extension often need scaffolding well into Grade 2.

## Lesson 5: Lead the Number Parade

| Primary expectations | C1.4 |

**Big idea:** Pattern rules also work with numbers — we can build repeating number parades up to 50 and explain the rule.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to use pattern rules with whole numbers up to 50, build a number parade, and explain its rule.

**Student learning goal:** I can build a repeating number parade up to 50, name its rule, and lead it.

**Success criteria:**
- I can use the rule from an animal parade to build a number parade up to 50.
- I can write at least 6 numbers in my parade strip in the correct order.

**Vocabulary introduced:** —
**Vocabulary reinforced:** pattern, core, pattern rule, predict, extend, translate, justify

- Optional: small paper crowns or 'Conductor' name tags for the leading phase

### Minds On (10 min) — From Animals to Numbers

**Hook:** Coco wants to know — can pattern rules work with NUMBERS too? Today we will find out, and then YOU lead the parade.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up Coco. Beside him, lay the animal AAB strip and, parallel below it, the number AAB strip (2-2-5).
> 
> Say to students: 'All week we have parades with animals, sounds, bodies. Today Coco asks: can we make a parade with NUMBERS? Look — here is the animal parade: Bea-Bea-Finn-Bea-Bea-Finn. What is the rule?' (AAB.) 'Yes! Now look at the number parade right below it: 2, 2, 5, 2, 2, 5. Where is A and where is B?' Take responses. (A is 2, B is 5.) 'Same rule! AAB. We just translated animals into numbers.'
> 
> Point to the hundred chart. 'Numbers can be small like 2, or bigger — up to 50 today. The rule does not care HOW big the numbers are, only the ORDER and how they REPEAT.' Quick demo: build a number parade using 10 and 25. 'A is 10, B is 25. AAB rule means: 10, 10, 25, 10, 10, 25. Read it with me.' Class reads.
> 
> 'Today YOU are the Conductor. You pick a rule. You pick numbers. You build a parade up to 50. You lead it. By end of class, every one of you is a Junior Pattern Conductor.' Smile. Hold up a hidden corner of one certificate.

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a circle on the carpet, facing the two parallel parade strips.
- Read the animal parade and name its rule (AAB).
- Read the number parade and identify A and B.
- Watch the teacher build a fresh number parade with bigger numbers (10 and 25).

### Action (30 min) — Plan, Build, Translate, Lead

*Structure:* `individual`

**Step 1.** Phase 1 — PLAN (5 min). Hand out the summative worksheet. Each child works independently at their desk. They pick a rule (AB, ABB, AAB, or ABC) and write it in the 'My Rule' box at the top. They draw or write the CORE in the 'My Core' box (a small loop showing the repeating part — e.g., for AAB, draw two animal silhouettes and one different).

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Conductors — first you PLAN. In the My Rule box write the letters of your rule. In the My Core box draw or write the part that will repeat. Be sure your rule and your core match. Take 5 minutes.'

**Step 2.** Phase 2 — BUILD an animal parade (8 min). Children draw or sticker their animal parade across an 8-cell strip on the worksheet, repeating their core. They must extend forward enough to fill all 8 cells. Then they LOOP the core (draw an oval around the first repetition).

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now BUILD. Use your rule to fill all 8 cells with animals. Loop the FIRST core — the part that starts to repeat. Read your parade quietly to yourself to check.' Begin one-on-one rubric conferences during this phase — ask each child to read their parade aloud and ask 'what would cell 9 be?'

**Step 3.** Phase 3 — TRANSLATE to a number parade (8 min). Below the animal parade, an empty 8-cell strip. Children pick TWO numbers between 1 and 50 (one for A, one for B; for ABC rules, three numbers). They write the two-or-three chosen numbers in a small KEY box, then fill the 8-cell strip with numbers using the SAME rule as the animal parade above. Hundred chart available for reference — copying numbers is fine.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now TRANSLATE. Pick TWO numbers between 1 and 50 — your choice. Write A = ___ and B = ___ in the key. Then fill the number strip with the SAME rule as your animals. Look at the hundred chart if you need help writing a number.' Continue rubric conferences. By end of Phase 3 most children should be scored on at least 3 of 4 expectations.

**Step 4.** Phase 4 — LEAD (7 min). Children pair up on the carpet. Partner A reads their parade aloud (animal first, then number). Partner B has to: (1) name the rule, (2) predict cell 9 of both parades, and (3) say one thing they noticed. Then swap. Use this phase to confirm uncertain rubric scores and upgrade where Level 4 thinking shows up.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say to students: 'Now LEAD. Find a partner on the carpet. Conductor A — read your animal parade, then your number parade, out loud. Conductor B — name the rule, predict cell 9, and say one thing you notice. Swap. Both conductors get to lead.'

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Provide a pre-printed AB rule on the worksheet so the child only chooses the elements (animals + numbers). Provide a number bank of small numbers (1, 2, 5, 10, 20) so the child does not have to invent. Pair with a confident peer for the lead phase.
- **For extending learners:** Challenge the child to use an ABC rule with three different animals AND three different numbers, including at least one number greater than 30. - **For ELL / IEP:** Provide a worksheet with the animal cells pre-numbered 1–8 below them so the child can visually align the rule. Provide a sentence frame for the lead phase: 'My rule is ___. My numbers are ___ and ___.' Accept gestures and pointing as part of the lead.

### Expanded walkthrough — The Number Parade Reveal — Translating to Numbers

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* C1.4 is the unique Grade 1 expectation: using repeating patterns to describe relationships among whole numbers up to 50. Children have practiced animal, sound, and body translations all week. Numbers are the abstract leap. The reveal needs to make explicit that the rule does not change when the things change, even when the things are numbers. Without this anchoring, children may treat the number parade as a counting exercise rather than a pattern exercise.

**Setup:** Two parallel parade strips on the carpet, both showing the AAB rule: Strip 1 = Bea-Bea-Finn-Bea-Bea-Finn animal version, Strip 2 = 2-2-5-2-2-5 number version (numbers written large on cards or directly on a laminated strip). A Core Loop Frame ready. The hundred chart visible on the math wall. Coco the puppet visible. Anchor chart with PATTERN, CORE, RULE, JUSTIFY all up.

**Teacher script:**

> Hold up Coco. Say to students: 'All week our parades had animals, sounds, bodies. Coco's last question for the week is the biggest one — can a parade be made of NUMBERS?'
> 
> Point to Strip 1 (animals). 'Read this parade with me.' Lead the class: 'Bea, Bea, Finn, Bea, Bea, Finn.' Place the loop around the first Bea-Bea-Finn group. 'Core?' (Bea-Bea-Finn.) 'Rule?' (AAB.) Confirm.
> 
> Now point to Strip 2 (numbers, parallel below). 'Now this parade. Read with me.' Lead: '2, 2, 5, 2, 2, 5.' Place the loop around the first 2-2-5 group. 'Core?' (2-2-5.) 'Rule?' Wait. The class should land on AAB. Validate. 'Same rule. Two parades, same rule.'
> 
> Pause. Step back. 'A is the bear. A is also the 2. They are not the same THING — but they play the same ROLE in the rule. B is the fox. B is also the 5. Same role.'
> 
> Pick up a fresh card. 'I want to show you something powerful. Let's change A. A is now 10. B is now 25. Same AAB rule. What does the parade look like?' Take responses. Build it: 10, 10, 25, 10, 10, 25. 'Read it.' Class reads. 'And the rule is still…?' (AAB.)
> 
> Final stretch — point to the hundred chart. 'Today on your worksheet you can pick any two numbers from this chart, anywhere up to 50, and use them as A and B. The rule does the work. The rule does not care if A is 2 or 47. The rule just says: A, A, B, A, A, B. You are the Conductor. You pick the numbers. You build the parade.'

**Expected student responses:**
- Many children will recognize the AAB rule instantly in the number parade after a week of practice.
- Some children will get confused by the SIZE of the numbers (e.g., asking 'is 50 too big?'). Reassure: any number on the chart is fine.
- A child may try to make a COUNTING pattern (1, 2, 3, 4) instead of a REPEATING pattern. This is a category mistake — counting up is a growing pattern (Grade 2). Redirect: 'Today we are doing REPEATING. Pick two numbers and let them repeat.'
- A child may pick the same number for A and B (e.g., A = 5, B = 5). Validate the math instinct, then redirect: 'If A and B are the same, the parade is just one number over and over — that's fine, but it does not show the rule. Pick two DIFFERENT numbers so the rule shines.'
- A child may invent an interesting twist (e.g., A = 10, B = 20, an ABBA rule). Celebrate and let them write it on the worksheet as a stretch.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Confusing repeating patterns with counting patterns — a child writes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 in the number strip thinking they have made a pattern. Counting up is a growing pattern (Grade 2 territory).
- Treating the size of the number as part of the rule — 'this is an AAB rule with small numbers and that is an AAB rule with big numbers, so they are different rules'. They are the same rule.
- Picking two numbers but not using them in AAB order — placing them randomly. Loop the core to redirect.
- Stopping at 6 cells because the animal parade was 6 cells, even though the worksheet has 8.

**Recovery moves:**
- If a child writes a counting pattern: have them point to the rule on their own worksheet ('Your rule is AAB. Where is the A? Where is the B? In a counting pattern, every cell is different — that's not your rule.'). Then start over with the worksheet's number bank.
- If a child treats number size as part of the rule: build two AAB parades side by side, one with (1, 2) and one with (40, 50). Read both. Ask 'what is the rule of each?' (AAB both.) 'See — same rule. Size does not matter.'
- If a child places numbers randomly: hand them the loop and say 'show me the core'. They cannot. Ask them to write A under each cell where A goes and B under each cell where B goes — first. Then write the numbers.
- If a child stops short of 8 cells: 'Your worksheet wants 8 cells. Use the rule to fill the rest. What is cell 7? What is cell 8?'

### Overall prep notes

Total prep time across all 7 assets: approximately 105 minutes for a class of 20 — slightly more than Kindergarten because Grade 1 pairs use sound/movement cards directly in Lesson 3 (5 partner sets vs K's 1 teacher set). Default print spec is BLACK AND WHITE for every asset — this ensures any printer in any classroom can produce the unit. Colour printing is suggested in several assets (M1, M3, Coco) for visual richness only; the unit works equally well in B&W because animal vests use distinct pattern fills (solid / stripes / dots / checkered) and Coco is line art. Recommended prep approach: prep the entire unit's manipulatives in one sitting before Lesson 1 — front-loads the work but means the unit runs smoothly day-to-day. Critical: laminate M2 parade strips because Lesson 5 requires writing numbers on them with dry-erase markers. Storage: keep all sets in a labelled bin or shelf, with each pair's set in its own bag. The animal cards (M1) are by far the most-used asset — laminate these even if you skip lamination on others.

Creates a repeating pattern with two elements when given a partner and a starter. | Confidently identifies, describes, and predicts in repeating patterns of 2 or 3 elements (animal, sound, shape, or letter). Independently creates a repeating pattern with two or three elements and explains the repeating part. | Spontaneously identifies repeating patterns in the classroom environment and across representations (numbers, letters, shapes, objects). Creates patterns with 3- or 4-element cores and predicts elements far beyond what is shown using the rule. |
| **C1.2** — create and translate patterns using objects, sounds, movements, and pictures | Treats different representations of the same rule as different patterns. Surface-feature thinking dominates ('they are not the same because one has bears and one has claps'). | With teacher modelling, can produce one second representation of a given rule, but the structure often drifts (e.g., ABB animal turns into AABB sound). Names what changed but not what stayed the same. | Independently translates a given rule (AB, ABB, AAB, or ABC) into 2 different representations from objects, sounds, movements, or pictures. Articulates that the rule stayed the same and only the things changed. | Translates one rule into 3 or more representations including invented ones (e.g., shapes, letters, numbers). Uses the rule label spontaneously when explaining and offers the same translation back to a new rule on the spot. |
| **C1.3** — determine pattern rules and use them to extend patterns, make and justify predictions, and identify missing elements in repeating patterns | Cannot consistently isolate the core or name a rule. Fills missing cells with random animals. Cannot justify a prediction beyond a guess ('I just thought it'). | Identifies the core in simple AB patterns and labels it AB with prompting. Fills missing elements at the end of a pattern. Justifies with surface description ('because the bear comes back') rather than rule language. | Independently identifies the core in AB, ABB, AAB, and ABC patterns and names the rule. Fills missing elements anywhere in the pattern (including the start, with some prompting). Justifies predictions using rule language ('because the rule is ABB'). | Names rules invented by classmates immediately. Extends patterns in both directions independently, including reasoning backward from a single given cell. Justifies predictions far beyond the visible pattern (e.g., 'cell 20 would be Bea because the rule is ABB and 20 divided by 3 lands on the A'). |
| **C1.4** — create and describe patterns to illustrate relationships among whole numbers up to 50 | Does not yet translate an animal parade into a number parade. May write random or counting numbers (1, 2, 3) instead of repeating numbers. Does not connect rule labels to numbers. | With teacher prompting, builds a 2-element repeating number parade using small numbers (1–10). Describes the rule using letter labels but not the relationship between the numbers. | Independently builds a repeating number parade up to 50 using two or three numbers and a chosen rule. Explains how the numbers in the parade follow the rule (e.g., 'A is 10 and B is 25 and the rule AB makes them take turns'). | Builds repeating number parades using larger numbers within 50 and ABC or invented rules. Predicts numbers in cells far beyond what is shown using the rule and the relationship between A, B, and C. |

Level 3 is the grade-level expectation; aim for most children to land at L2 or L3 by end-of-unit. L1 is a signal for additional support; L4 is a signal for enrichment in Grade 2. Communicate scores to families using descriptive language alongside the level (e.g., 'Achieving Level 3 — independently builds and explains repeating patterns with a chosen rule, including using whole numbers up to 50') rather than letter grades.

### Lead the Number Parade — Summative Administration Guide (Grade 1)

*Ties the Lesson 5 worksheet, the lesson plan's four phases (Plan-Build-Translate-Lead), and the rubric together. Tells the teacher exactly what evidence to look for during each phase and which expectation each phase's evidence maps to.*

#### Administration steps

1. Before the lesson: photocopy one summative worksheet ('Lead the Number Parade') per child. Print one rubric per child or maintain a single class rubric on a clipboard with student-name rows pre-filled. Have the Junior Pattern Conductor certificates printed and ready for the closing ceremony (do not show them to children before they have earned them). Confirm the hundred chart 0–50 is visible on the math wall.
2. Phase 1 (Plan, 5 min): Children write their rule (AB/ABB/AAB/ABC) in My Rule and draw their core in My Core. This produces visible evidence for C1.3 (rule identification). Walk the room and check that each child has a rule AND a matching core; nudge children whose rule and core do not match.
3. Phase 2 (Build animal parade, 8 min): Children fill the 8-cell animal parade and loop the first core. This produces visible evidence for C1.1 (creates a pattern using the rule) and confirms C1.3. Begin one-on-one rubric conferences during this phase — ask each child to read their parade aloud and answer 'what would cell 9 be? How do you know?'.
4. Phase 3 (Translate to numbers, 8 min): Children fill the My Number Key (A = ___, B = ___, optional C = ___) with numbers 1–50 and complete the 8-cell number parade using the same rule. This produces evidence for C1.4 (number parade up to 50) AND C1.2 (translating to a different representation). Continue rubric conferences. By end of Phase 3 you should have scored most children on at least 3 of 4 expectations.
5. Phase 4 (Lead, 7 min): Children pair up on the carpet and lead each other through their parade — animals first, then numbers. Listen for partners articulating the rule, predicting what comes next in both parades, and explaining the relationship between the animals and the numbers. Use this phase to confirm uncertain rubric scores and upgrade scores where Level 4 thinking shows up.
6. After the lesson: review the rubric with the worksheet artifact in front of you. For any child below Level 3 on any expectation, note what evidence (or absence) drove that decision. Use this for the report-card narrative. Also review the bottom-of-worksheet justify sentence — a child who wrote (or had scribed) a rule-referenced explanation has Level 3 evidence on C1.3 even if their oral conference was tentative.

**Scoring guidance:** Use the worksheet as primary evidence and the partner-lead conversation as confirming evidence. For each expectation, ask: 'What level of independence and confidence did this child show?' Level 3 is the grade-level goal — aim for most children to land at L2 or L3 by end-of-unit. Children who are L4 on every expectation are ready for Grade 2 pattern work (growing and shrinking patterns); document this for the receiving teacher. Children who are L1 on multiple expectations need targeted small-group work in the next math block, especially around the new Grade 1 vocabulary (rule, justify) and the number-parade translation.

#### Evidence per expectation

- **C1.1** — Look at Part 2 of the worksheet (animal parade). Did the child create a valid 8-cell repeating pattern using their chosen rule? Combined with their My Core box. A child who fills Part 2 with a coherent repetition of their rule is at Level 3 on C1.1. A child who also uses 3-element cores (ABB, AAB, ABC) without prompting is at Level 4.
- **C1.2** — Look at Part 3 (number parade). Does the number parade use the SAME structural rule as the animal parade in Part 2? A child who fills Part 3 with numbers that follow the same rule (e.g., AAB animal becomes AAB number) is at Level 3 on C1.2 — they have translated. A child who drifts in structure (animal AAB becomes number ABB) is at Level 2.
- **C1.3** — Look at Part 1 (My Rule and My Core boxes) AND the bottom-of-page justify sentence. Did the child write a rule label that matches their core? Did they justify with rule language ('because the rule is ___')? Combined with the partner-lead conversation in Phase 4 ('how do you know cell 9 is ___?'). A child who writes both correctly AND justifies aloud with rule language is at Level 3.
- **C1.4** — Look at the My Number Key in Part 3. Did the child pick numbers between 1 and 50 (not just 1–10)? Did the number parade preserve the rule? A child who picks any two valid numbers between 1 and 50 and builds the parade correctly is at Level 3. A child who only used very small numbers (1, 2) is at Level 2 (the relationship is correct but the up-to-50 range is unproven). A child who used larger numbers AND can explain the relationship between A and B as numbers (e.g., 'A is 10 and B is 25, B is bigger') is at Level 4.

Take patterns from Kindergarten noticing into Grade 1 reasoning. The Pattern Parade — Grade 1 Edition is a 5-day, ready-to-teach unit that takes children on a journey with Coco the Conductor and the Parade Animals — Bea the Bear, Finn the Fox, Bibi the Beaver, and Moss the Moose — to name pattern rules, translate them across representations, justify their predictions, and finally build repeating number parades using whole numbers up to 50.

Children build animal parades with manipulatives, name their rules using letter labels, translate one rule into multiple representations (animal, sound, body, number), find missing elements at the start, middle, AND end of broken parades, justify their predictions out loud, and finally lead their own number parade as Junior Pattern Conductors.

### Pedagogical approach

Active, oral-first, movement-rich, with a Grade 1 emphasis on naming rules and justifying predictions out loud. Worksheets are end-of-lesson consolidation, never the main activity.
