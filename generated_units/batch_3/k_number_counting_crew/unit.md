## Unit overview

A 5-day Kindergarten unit on Number Sense and Operations, framed around 'The Counting Crew' — Mae and Theo, two child chefs working in the classroom kitchen with their counting companion Buddy the puppy. Children explore why numbers exist (Day 1), discover that counting forward grows quantity and counting backward shrinks it (Day 2), recognise and write 0–20 (Day 3), subitize small groups instantly without counting (Day 4), and finally compose/decompose numbers and compare groups using MORE / LESS / EQUAL (Day 5). The unit progresses from concrete (linking cubes) to representational (dot cards, number cards) to abstract (written numerals + comparison language). Classroom-Canadian context throughout.

**Unit learning goal:** Children will explore why numbers are used, count forward and backward, recognise and write numbers to 20, subitize quantities to 5, compose and decompose numbers to 10, and compare quantities as more, less, or equal.

### Curriculum expectations

- **A6.1** — explore what numbers are used for
- **A6.2** — understand when counting forward, the quantity gets bigger and when counting backward, the quantity gets smaller
- **A6.3** — identify and write numbers to 20
- **A6.4** — subitize quantities to 5
- **A6.5** — compose and decompose quantities to 10
- **A6.6** — understand the concept of more than, the same (equal), or less than

### Recurring characters

- **Mae** — Lead Counter — counts forward and backward, models writing numbers 0-20
  *Visual:* A young child chef in a white apron and chef hat, holding a small loaf of bread. Friendly, expressive face. Chosen to centre the unit on real-world counting in a classroom 'kitchen' context.
- **Theo** — Mae's Cooking Partner — models composing/decomposing groups of ingredients
  *Visual:* A young child chef in a white apron and chef hat, holding a wooden spoon and a small mixing bowl. Friendly, expressive face. Pairs with Mae for partner-counting moments.
- **Buddy** — Counting Companion — appears alongside the kids; subitizing examples often use Buddy's bones, paw prints, etc.
  *Visual:* A friendly golden-retriever puppy sitting alertly with a dog bone beside it. Used in subitizing dot cards (1-5 paw prints, 1-5 bones).

### Threaded vocabulary

- **count** — saying numbers in order to find out 'how many'.
- **more** — a bigger amount.
- **less** — a smaller amount.
- **equal** — the same amount, no more and no less.
- **subitize** — knowing how many WITHOUT counting one by one — like seeing 3 dots and just knowing it's three.
- **compose** — putting two smaller groups together to make a bigger group (4 and 3 makes 7).
- **decompose** — breaking a group into two smaller groups (7 can be 4 and 3, or 5 and 2).

- Linking counting cubes (~30 per child)
- Laminated ten-frame mats
- Subitizing dot-card set (1–5 dots, 10 cards)
- Number cards 0–20 (set per child)
- Comparison strip (per pair)

### Assessment strategy

- **Diagnostic:** Lesson 1 number-hunt observation: which children identify ≥3 number-uses around the room? Which connect 'how many'?
- **Formative:** Lesson 4 subitize-and-tell: tally per child how many of 5 dot-cards they identify without counting. Mid-unit formative worksheet between L3 and L4 captures number-recognition fluency 0-20.
- **Summative:** Lesson 5 worksheet — compose 7 in three different ways, then judge two plates of cookies as MORE / LESS / EQUAL with a justification picture/word. Captures A6.5 + A6.6 mastery.

### Pedagogical notes

- SEL/A1 woven throughout: every lesson has a partner-share moment so children practise listening + waiting their turn while counting aloud.
- Concrete-Representational-Abstract progression: cubes (concrete) → dot cards / number cards (representational) → written numerals + comparison symbols (abstract).
- Teen numbers (11–19) are a known stumbling block at K. Lesson 3 dedicates explicit teacher prompts to 'thirteen vs thirty' style confusions.
- Subitizing capped at 5 — the Ontario K expectation. Children who instantly recognise 6+ are noted for the Grade-1 stretch.
- Counting backward (Lesson 2) often surfaces the misconception that 'counting' only means going up. The Counting Path floor mat makes the bidirectional move physical and embodied.

### Canadian context notes

- Counting objects in Lesson 1 include classroom maple-leaf cards, wooden puzzle pieces, and toonies/loonies — everyday Canadian classroom artefacts.
- Lesson 2 'countdown to recess' uses the school day's actual snack/recess timer.
- Lesson 5 cookie-plate comparison uses Tim Hortons-style timbits as the visual reference — a recognisable Canadian everyday food.

## Lesson 1: What Are Numbers For?

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Kindergarten |
| Strand | A. Foundations of Language and Mathematics — Number Sense and Operations |
| Duration | 40 minutes |
| Primary expectations | A6.1 |
| Secondary expectations | — |

**Big idea:** Numbers help us answer 'how many', label things, measure stuff, and tell the time — they're everywhere in our classroom and our world.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning that numbers are used for many different jobs, not just counting — they label things, measure things, and tell us when things happen.

**Student learning goal:** I can find places where numbers are used in our classroom and tell why they matter.

**Success criteria:**
- I can find at least three different numbers around the classroom.
- I can say what each number is for (counting, naming, measuring, or telling time).
- I can name one job that numbers do in our every day.

**Vocabulary introduced:** count, number, label, measure
**Vocabulary reinforced:** —
**Manipulatives used:** M1_counting_cubes, M5_counting_objects_set

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Anchor chart paper with four labelled columns: COUNTING — LABELS — MEASURE — TIME
- Marker for the anchor chart
- One small clipboard per partner pair (~12 clipboards) with a printed 'Number Hunt' tracking sheet
- One pencil per child
- Coco-the-Conductor-style classroom 'kitchen' apron for Mae and Theo (puppet/character introduction)
- Optional: a wall clock, a wall calendar, a measuring tape — pre-positioned so children can find them

### Minds On (10 min) — Where Are the Numbers?

**Hook:** Today we welcome two new friends to our classroom: Mae and Theo, the Counting Crew chefs. They cook delicious snacks — but every recipe they make needs NUMBERS. They need YOUR help to find numbers all over the room.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up the Mae illustration in one hand and the Theo illustration in the other. 'Friends, meet Mae and Theo. They are the Counting Crew chefs in our classroom kitchen. They are baking cookies today, and they need a lot of numbers.
> 
>  How many cups of flour? How many cookies on the tray? How LONG to bake them? Without numbers, the cookies would not work.' Pause for a chuckle. Continue: 'But Mae and Theo just got here, and they don't know where the numbers in our classroom are. Look around — can you point to a number you can see right now?' Wait for hands. Take 3-4 quick answers, pointing or walking children to each spot. Listen for: clock, calendar, number line, page numbers in books, wall measuring chart, recycling-bin labels, ruler. As each is named, tape a sticky note on or near it that says NUMBER FOUND. The carpet should slowly fill with sticky-noted spots around the room. End: 'Numbers are EVERYWHERE in our classroom — and every single one of them has a JOB. Today our job is to find as many numbers as we can, and figure out what each one is FOR.'

**Student actions:**
- Listen and watch as Mae and Theo are introduced.
- Look around the room and raise hand when they spot a number.
- Walk to the spot they pointed at when called on, and place the sticky note.
- Notice how many sticky notes pile up by the end of Minds On.

### Action (20 min) — The Number Hunt

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole group: introduce the Number Hunt clipboard. Show the tracking sheet with four boxes labelled COUNTING (cookies, fingers), LABELS (page numbers, recycling-bin numbers), MEASURE (rulers, weight scales, measuring tape), TIME (clock hands, calendar dates). Walk to one already-noticed number (e.g., the wall clock) and ask: 'What's this number for?' Take ideas, then sort it into TIME together. Repeat with one more (e.g., a page number in a picture book) — sort into LABELS.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say: 'Friends, your job is to be number detectives. Find a number, then ASK YOURSELF — is this for COUNTING, for LABELS, for MEASURING, or for TIME? Then draw or write where you found it in the right box.'

**Step 2.** Send partner pairs out with one clipboard between them. Pairs walk the classroom (and the hallway if safe) and find at least four different numbers, one for each box on their tracking sheet. They draw a quick sketch in the box (or write the spot if they can spell it).

> *Teacher prompt:* Walk pairs to their starting points around the room. Remind: 'You are looking for FOUR DIFFERENT JOBS — not just four counting examples. Look high up, look down low, look on the wall, look in your cubby.'

**Step 3.** Circulate and prompt with the diagnostic checklist. Watch for children who default to ALL counting examples (most common at K) — gently redirect them to look for time, labels, or measurement. Note which children spontaneously identify a non-counting use.

> *Teacher prompt:* Drop down to a pair's level. 'Show me what you have so far. Hmm — these are all counting things. Where in the room could you find a number that tells the TIME?' If they shrug, point at the clock, the calendar. 'And a measuring number?' Point at the height chart, the rain-stick markings, your ruler.

**Step 4.** Pairs return to the carpet with their clipboards. Anchor chart on the easel with the same four columns. Each pair shares ONE find — they tell where they found the number, and the class decides together which column it goes in. Teacher records on the chart with marker (write the spot, draw a tiny icon).

> *Teacher prompt:* Say: 'Pair-A, share your tricky one — the one you weren't sure about. Where did you find it, and what column should it go in?' Take pair contributions one at a time. If the class disagrees, GREAT — discuss WHY (e.g., the number on a school bus is BOTH a label AND a count of which bus number it is).

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Emerging children walk with a stronger partner; teacher hands them a 'starter card' showing one example per column to spark recognition. They contribute by pointing or naming, not necessarily writing.
- **For extending learners:** Extending children find a FIFTH job a number can do that isn't on the chart (phone numbers, addresses, room numbers — these are typically labels but can prompt rich discussion). They start a fifth column on the back of the clipboard.
- **For ELL / IEP:** Provide picture-card prompts for each column header (clock-and-watch, page-number-and-book, ruler-and-tape, calendar-and-bus-number). Pre-teach 'count', 'label', 'measure', 'time' with hand gestures the child can echo while walking the room. Allow drawing instead of writing.
- **For movement learners:** The whole lesson IS the movement opportunity — pairs walk while hunting. For children who need additional regulation, they can carry a clipboard AND a small sandbag they place beside their spot when they find one.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Diagnostic-only at this stage. Note (a) which children spontaneously look beyond counting, (b) which children need redirection to non-counting examples, (c) which children can articulate the JOB a number does without prompting. These three signals predict where each child sits on the A6.1 progression entering Lesson 2.
- **How to record:** Class diagnostic checklist with rows for each child and three columns: 'identifies non-counting uses', 'articulates job', 'collaborates with partner on the hunt'. Tick as you observe.
- **Tracker columns:** identifies_non_counting, articulates_job, partner_collaboration

### Consolidation (10 min) — Counting Crew Conversation

**Discussion prompts:**
- Mae just asked us — could she bake cookies WITHOUT numbers? What would happen?
- Theo wants to know — what was the most surprising number you found today? Where was it, and what was it for?
- If our classroom did not have ANY numbers in it, what would be different? What would still work? What would break?

**Exit routine:** On the way out the door, each child names ONE place in the room where they saw a number AND says one of the four jobs (counting, label, measure, time). Teacher tallies the four jobs to see if the class touched all of them or skewed to counting.

### Assessment (diagnostic)

- **Evidence collected:** Anchor chart populated with class finds (collective evidence of which number-jobs the class recognised). Per-child diagnostic checklist with three indicators per child. Exit-routine tally showing distribution across the four jobs.
- **Tracker:** `L1_diagnostic_tracker`
- **Rationale:** A6.1 'explore what numbers are used for' is the BASELINE expectation for the unit. Children entering K vary widely — some can articulate that a clock-number means time; others see all numbers as counting. This lesson tests that baseline before Lessons 2-5 build on it.

### Expanded walkthrough — When Every Find Is 'Counting' — redirecting toward labels, measurement, and time

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* The most common K trap on A6.1 is for the entire class to find only COUNTING examples — fingers, blocks, cookies, paper bears. The richer expectation is recognising that numbers ALSO label, measure, and tell time. Step 3 (circulate during the partner hunt) is where this redirect happens. The script below covers what to say and which physical objects to point at when a pair has filled all four boxes with counting examples.

**Setup:** Each pair has a Number Hunt clipboard with four boxes (COUNTING / LABELS / MEASURE / TIME). The teacher carries a diagnostic checklist clipboard and a pen. The classroom should have at least one VISIBLE example for each of the four jobs: a wall clock (TIME), a measuring tape or height chart (MEASURE), a recycling-bin number sticker or page numbers in a picture book (LABELS), and any quantity at all (COUNTING). If your room is sparse, pre-position one of each before Minds On.

**Teacher script:**

> Walk to a pair whose clipboard shows four counting examples (e.g., 'fingers — 5', 'cookies — 6', 'cubes — 10', 'bears — 4'). Crouch to their level. 'Wow, four numbers — great hunting. Tell me what each one is for.' Wait. They will probably say 'counting' for all four. Affirm: 'Yes, those are all counting numbers. Let us see if we can find a number that does a DIFFERENT job. Look up — can you see the clock?' Most children will. 'What numbers are on the clock?' They might say 1 through 12. 'And what is the clock telling us right now?' Wait for 'the time' or a guess. 'Yes — the clock uses numbers to tell us TIME. That is a different job from counting.' Have them draw the clock in the TIME box. Now point to a picture book on the table. Open it. 'See this little number in the corner of every page? That is a PAGE NUMBER. It tells us the page name — like a label. It is not counting how MANY pages we have read; it is NAMING which page we are on.' Have them draw a tiny page in the LABELS box. Move to MEASURE. Walk them to the height chart on the wall. 'Stand here. The numbers tell us how tall you are. That is MEASURING — they tell us SIZE, not just how many.' Have them draw the chart in the MEASURE box. Step back. 'Now your clipboard has all FOUR jobs. Tell me again — what jobs do numbers do?' Listen for the four words: count, label, measure, time. If they get all four, tick all three diagnostic columns on your checklist. If they hesitate on one, they are emerging on A6.1 — note that and circle back in Lessons 4 and 5.

**Expected student responses:**
- (Confused) Identifies four counting examples and says 'they are all for counting' — has not yet generalised that numbers do other jobs.
- (Mid) After you walk them to the clock, they grasp TIME quickly but still need help finding LABELS or MEASURE.
- (Strong) On their own, they articulate 'pages have names that are numbers — that is not counting'. Gets all four jobs unprompted.
- (Stretched) Volunteers a fifth job — 'phone numbers are labels for people'. Recognises that one number can serve multiple jobs.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Every number is a counting number. (Child cannot yet separate quantity from label or measure or time. Most common K starting point.)
- The clock numbers ARE counting (counting up to 12). (Child correctly notes 1-12 increases but does not grasp that the clock USES that sequence to tell TIME.)
- Page numbers count how many pages you have read. (Child interprets the numeral as a quantity rather than a label or identifier.)

**Recovery moves:**
- Walk the child to a non-counting example physically. Do not describe it from the carpet — show them the clock face, the page corner, the height chart. Embodiment beats explanation at K.
- Use the JOB framing every time. 'What JOB is this number doing?' Not 'how many is this?' — that question reverts the child to counting.
- Anchor chart pairing. After redirecting one pair to the clock, take a photo with your phone of the clipboard with the new TIME entry. Add it to the anchor chart so the whole class sees the redirect succeed.

### Student worksheet brief — Where Did You See a Number?

*End-of-lesson consolidation. Children record one number-find per box on a four-quadrant page (COUNTING / LABELS / MEASURE / TIME). Captures the diagnostic-level evidence for A6.1 and creates a take-home conversation starter ('Show your family the four kinds of numbers you found today').*

## Lesson 2: Forward and Backward

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Kindergarten |
| Strand | A. Foundations of Language and Mathematics — Number Sense and Operations |
| Duration | 40 minutes |
| Primary expectations | A6.2 |
| Secondary expectations | A6.1 |

**Big idea:** When we count FORWARD, the amount gets BIGGER. When we count BACKWARD, the amount gets SMALLER. The direction we count tells us if we are growing the amount or shrinking it.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning that counting forward makes the amount bigger and counting backward makes the amount smaller, and we are using our feet on the Counting Path to feel the change.

**Student learning goal:** I can count forward and backward and tell what happens to the amount.

**Success criteria:**
- I can step forward on the Counting Path and say each number out loud (0, 1, 2, 3 …).
- I can step backward on the Counting Path and say each number out loud (5, 4, 3, 2 …).
- I can tell my partner whether the amount got BIGGER or SMALLER after I stepped.

**Vocabulary introduced:** forward, backward, bigger, smaller
**Vocabulary reinforced:** count, number, more, less
**Manipulatives used:** M7_counting_path, M1_counting_cubes, M5_counting_objects_set

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Counting Path floor mat (numbered 0–20 with footprints), unrolled in an open carpet area
- A small stack of linking cubes (about 10) per partner pair
- One small basket of paper apples (M5) for the recess countdown demo
- Two arrow cards: a green FORWARD arrow and a red BACKWARD arrow, large enough to hold up at the carpet
- A snack-time timer or kitchen timer for the 'countdown to recess' moment
- Anchor chart paper labelled 'FORWARD = BIGGER · BACKWARD = SMALLER' with two columns

### Minds On (10 min) — Mae's Recess Countdown

**Hook:** Mae and Theo are baking apple muffins, but they keep peeking at the timer because RECESS is coming. Today the Counting Crew will teach us a special chef trick: counting BACKWARD when we are waiting for something exciting.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up Mae and Theo. 'Friends, Mae and Theo started baking muffins twelve minutes ago. They keep asking — how long until recess?' Hold up five paper apples in a basket. 'Yesterday we counted UP — one apple, two apples, three apples — to find HOW MANY. The amount got BIGGER each time. Watch.'
> 
>  Add an apple as you count: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. 'Forward counting makes the amount BIGGER.' Hold up the green FORWARD arrow. Now take an apple OUT of the basket. 'But what if we are waiting? Like waiting for recess? Then we count BACKWARD. Five … four … three … two … one … RECESS!' Take an apple out on each number. The basket empties. 'Backward counting makes the amount SMALLER.' Hold up the red BACKWARD arrow. 'Today on the Counting Path, your feet will feel the difference.'

**Student actions:**
- Watch Mae and Theo set up the apple basket.
- Count along 1-2-3-4-5 as the teacher adds apples (forward).
- Count along 5-4-3-2-1 as the teacher takes apples out (backward).
- Point to the green or red arrow when the teacher asks 'which direction?'.

### Action (20 min) — The Counting Path — Forward and Back

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole group at the Counting Path. The teacher demonstrates: stand on 0, then step forward one footprint at a time, calling out each number — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Hold up a small tower of cubes; add one cube on each step so children see the amount grow. Stop on 5 and ask: 'Did the cubes get bigger or smaller?' Children chorus 'BIGGER'. Then turn around and step BACKWARD from 5 to 0, removing a cube on each step. Stop on 0. 'Did the cubes get bigger or smaller?' Children chorus 'SMALLER'.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say: 'Watch my feet. When I step toward the green arrow, I'm counting FORWARD — and look, my cube tower is getting bigger and bigger. When I step toward the red arrow, I'm counting BACKWARD — and my tower is getting smaller and smaller. The DIRECTION my feet go tells us what happens to the amount.'

**Step 2.** Partner pairs take the Counting Path two at a time (others watch and chant). Partner A walks forward from 0 to 5 with a cube tower, building it up. Partner B walks backward from 5 to 0 with the same tower, taking cubes off. After each pair finishes, the watchers shout out 'BIGGER!' (forward) and 'SMALLER!' (backward) so the language sticks. Rotate every 90 seconds; aim for every child to take both directions at least once.

> *Teacher prompt:* Coach as needed: 'Slow your feet down — say the number on every footprint, not in between. If you skip a number, the cubes will skip too.' For a child who steps but stays silent, say the number with them: 'Together: ONE … TWO … THREE.'

**Step 3.** Mid-Action twist: 'mystery start'. Teacher places a child on a footprint that is NOT 0 (e.g., 7) and points at either the green or red arrow. The child has to step the right way and call out the next three numbers (e.g., starts at 7, green arrow → 8, 9, 10). This forces children to count from any starting point, not just from zero, and matches the A6.2 expectation that the SEQUENCE works in both directions everywhere on the line.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say: 'You don't always start at zero. You might start in the middle. Watch my arrow — green or red? Tell me your three next numbers BEFORE your feet move, then walk it.'

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Emerging children stay in the 0-to-5 range and walk with a stronger partner who narrates each number. The teacher physically taps each footprint as the child steps. Skip the 'mystery start' twist — finish the lesson on the basic forward-then-backward demonstration only.
- **For extending learners:** Extending children walk all the way to 10 (or 20 if confident). They also do 'skip-counting' demos by 2s on the path (0, 2, 4 …) and tell the class whether their cubes are still going BIGGER or SMALLER each time. Some can predict: 'If I'm on 8 and step FORWARD twice, where will I be?' before walking it.
- **For ELL / IEP:** Pre-teach the four target words with hand gestures: FORWARD (open hand pushing away from chest), BACKWARD (open hand pulling toward chest), BIGGER (hands wide apart), SMALLER (hands close together). Children echo the gesture every time the word is spoken. Provide a number line with arrows printed on the desk so they can finger-trace before foot-walking.
- **For movement learners:** The whole lesson is movement — these children will thrive. Offer them the role of 'arrow holder' between turns, walking the green or red arrow over to whoever's about to start. Bonus regulation: give them a small bean bag to carry that they pass to the next walker.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Three signals: (a) does the child say each number aloud as they step, or do they skip / stay silent? (b) does the child move feet in the direction the teacher's arrow points (green = forward, red = backward), or do they default to forward regardless? (c) when prompted 'is the amount BIGGER or SMALLER now?', does the child use the matched word, or stick to one word for both directions? Children who match the direction word to the arrow are secure on A6.2.
- **How to record:** Class formative checklist with rows for each child and three columns: 'narrates each number', 'matches feet to arrow', 'uses bigger/smaller correctly'. Tick during the partner walks; circle gaps for tomorrow's small-group recovery.
- **Tracker columns:** narrates_each_number, matches_direction_to_arrow, uses_bigger_smaller_correctly

### Consolidation (10 min) — Bigger or Smaller? Counting Crew Conversation

**Discussion prompts:**
- Mae just asked us — when she counts the muffins coming OUT of the oven, is that forward or backward? Why?
- Theo wants to know — if our timer says 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 … what is happening to the time we have left?
- Show me with your hands: was it BIGGER or SMALLER when you walked toward the red arrow on the Counting Path?

**Exit routine:** Each child names ONE direction word (forward or backward) and ONE size word (bigger or smaller) that go together. The teacher tallies pairings on the anchor chart. By the end the chart should clearly show FORWARD pairs with BIGGER and BACKWARD pairs with SMALLER.

### Assessment (formative)

- **Evidence collected:** Per-child formative checklist with three indicators recorded during the partner walks (narrates each number, matches feet to arrow, uses the correct size word). Anchor-chart tally from the exit routine showing how cleanly the class paired FORWARD with BIGGER and BACKWARD with SMALLER. Photo of the Counting Path with each child's name written on the highest footprint they reached.
- **Tracker:** `L2_formative_tracker`
- **Rationale:** A6.2 'understand when counting forward, the quantity gets bigger and when counting backward, the quantity gets smaller' is a directional concept — children must connect a SEQUENCE direction to a SIZE change. The formative tier is appropriate here because the unit will revisit bidirectional counting in Lesson 4 (subitizing) and Lesson 5 (composing/decomposing); this lesson establishes the bigger/smaller language that those later lessons depend on.

### Expanded walkthrough — When the Child Steps Backward but Says 'Bigger' — repairing the direction-to-size link

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* The most common K trap on A6.2 is for a child to walk the right direction but use the wrong size word. They've learned that COUNTING means BIGGER (from Lesson 1 and from years of being asked 'how many?'), so when they count 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 backward, the COUNTING habit overrides the BACKWARD direction and they still say 'bigger'. Step 2 (partner walks) is where the teacher catches this. The script below covers exactly what to say when a child walks backward but uses the wrong size word, and why the cube tower is the recovery tool.

**Setup:** Counting Path is unrolled. The pair has just finished — Partner B walked from 5 back to 0 with a cube tower, taking cubes off on each step. The tower started at five cubes and is now down to one (or zero). Partner B is standing on footprint 0 holding the small tower. Teacher steps in BEFORE the next pair starts.

**Teacher script:**

> Crouch beside Partner B. Hold up their tower next to where it started (five cubes high) — visualise the SHRINK with your hands. 'Tell me what happened to your cube tower when you walked backward.' If they say 'bigger', stay calm — this is the teaching moment. 'Hmm, let us look together. When you started, how many cubes did you have?' Hold up five fingers. 'And when you finished?' Hold up zero or one finger. 'So — did the tower get bigger, or smaller?' Wait. They will usually self-correct: 'smaller'. Affirm: 'YES — smaller. When your feet went BACKWARD, the cubes got SMALLER. Now let us say it together: BACKWARD makes the amount … SMALLER.' Have them echo with the hand gestures (hands closing in for SMALLER). Walk them through one more backward step, this time narrating with them: 'feet going backward … cubes going smaller'. If they STILL say 'bigger' even after the cube demo, it's not a misconception about direction — it's a habit-driven word slip. Move them to the green arrow side and have them count forward 0 to 3, then ask 'bigger or smaller?' (correct: bigger). Then send them backward 3 to 0, and ask again. The contrast between adjacent forward and backward walks usually breaks the habit. Tick the 'uses bigger/smaller correctly' column once they nail the contrast, even if it took two attempts. Note in the comments column: 'needed cube re-show' — that's a flag to give them a one-on-one bigger/smaller round at tomorrow's morning meeting before Lesson 3.

**Expected student responses:**
- (Confused) Walks backward but still says 'bigger' — the counting habit overrides the direction. Most common K starting point.
- (Mid) Says 'smaller' once you point at the cube tower, but defaults to 'bigger' again on the next backward walk without prompting.
- (Strong) On the second backward walk, says 'smaller' before the cubes are even removed, because they've internalised the direction-to-size link.
- (Stretched) Volunteers a real-life example: 'Like when there's only ONE muffin left — that's smaller than five muffins.' Generalises beyond the path.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Counting always makes the amount bigger. (Child has only ever counted up, so counting and growing are the same word in their head. Most common K starting point.)
- The numbers on the Counting Path are 'just labels' — stepping on 5 doesn't mean five of anything. (Child can recite 0-20 but does not connect each number to a quantity. The cube tower is the repair.)
- Backward = wrong. (Some children believe counting backward is a mistake — they self-correct mid-walk and start over from 0. Affirm that backward IS counting, just in a different direction.)

**Recovery moves:**
- Use the cube tower every time. Words alone don't repair this — children need to SEE the tower shrink in their hands while their feet step backward. Embody.
- Adjacent forward-then-backward walks. The contrast of 'just walked forward, cubes got bigger; now walk backward, cubes get smaller' breaks the habit faster than two separate backward attempts.
- Hand gestures every time. BIGGER (hands open wide), SMALLER (hands close in). Children remember the gesture-to-word link better than the word-to-direction link at this age.

Captures formative-level evidence for A6.2 and reinforces the direction-to-size pairing established on the floor mat. Goes home as a take-home explainer ('Show your family how the Counting Crew counts forward and backward').*

## Lesson 3: Number Letters

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Kindergarten |
| Strand | A. Foundations of Language and Mathematics — Number Sense and Operations |
| Duration | 40 minutes |
| Primary expectations | A6.3 |
| Secondary expectations | A6.1 |

**Big idea:** Each number has its own way of being written, like a special letter. We can READ the numbers from 0 to 20 and we can WRITE them too. The teen numbers (11-19) are tricky because they sound like a small number but get written with TWO digits.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to recognise and write the numbers from 0 to 20, paying special attention to the tricky teen numbers like 13 and 14 that sound a lot like 30 and 40 but look different on the page.

**Student learning goal:** I can read and write the numbers from 0 to 20.

**Success criteria:**
- I can match a number card (e.g., 7) to a group of seven counting objects.
- I can put the number cards 0–20 in order, even when 11, 12, 13 try to confuse me.
- I can trace and then write at least five numbers between 0 and 20 on my own.

**Vocabulary introduced:** numeral, teen, trace, match
**Vocabulary reinforced:** count, number, forward, backward
**Manipulatives used:** M4_number_cards, M1_counting_cubes, M5_counting_objects_set

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Number cards 0-20, one set per child (M4)
- A 'teen poster' with 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 in large print, with a 'tens-frame + extras' icon under each
- Tracing strips with dotted-line numerals 0-20, one strip per child
- Pencils and crayons (each child)
- Sticky tape or pegs to clip ordered cards onto a clothesline at child height
- A long piece of yarn or string strung across the carpet to act as the 'number clothesline' from 0 to 20
- Anchor chart paper labelled 'Tricky Teens' with columns 13 / 30, 14 / 40, 15 / 50, 16 / 60, 17 / 70, 18 / 80, 19 / 90

### Minds On (10 min) — The Mixed-Up Recipe Cards

**Hook:** Mae and Theo were writing down a new cookie recipe — but a gust of wind blew their number cards everywhere! They need OUR help to put the numbers back in order from 0 to 20 so they don't accidentally bake 30 cookies instead of 13.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet near the yarn 'clothesline'. Hold up a basket of number cards 0-20, all jumbled. 'Look, friends — Mae and Theo's recipe cards got blown out of order. The clothesline is empty. We need to peg every number from zero up to twenty, in the right order.
> 
>  But watch out — some numbers like to TRICK us. Listen: thirteen … thirty. Fourteen … forty. They sound almost the same. Today we are going to learn how to TELL THEM APART by looking at how they are WRITTEN.' Pull out card 13 and card 30. 'Thirteen has a 1 then a 3. Thirty has a 3 then a 0. Different LETTERS for different NUMBERS.' Tape the teen poster on the easel. 'These are the tricky teens — eleven through nineteen. They all start with a 1.' Then peg 0 on the far left of the clothesline. 'Who can come find ONE and peg it next?' Take volunteers for 0, 1, 2 to start the line.

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a half-circle facing the empty number clothesline.
- Listen as the teacher demonstrates 13 vs. 30 with the poster.
- Volunteer to peg one card on the clothesline when called.
- Chant the numbers 0, 1, 2, 3 … as each card is added so the order builds aloud.

### Action (20 min) — Number Stations — Match, Order, Trace

*Structure:* `centres`

**Step 1.** Set up three rotating stations. Station MATCH: a tray of number cards 0-20 mixed up beside small bowls — each bowl has a quantity (e.g., 7 paper bears, 13 cubes, 20 buttons). Children pick a card and find the matching bowl. Station ORDER: a child-height clothesline with pegs — children take a small set of cards (a 0-10 set OR an 11-20 set, depending on readiness) and clip them in order. Station TRACE: tracing strips and pencils — children trace each numeral 0-20, with extra-thick dotted lines on the teen numbers. Walk children through the rotation rules; each station gets ~6 minutes.

> *Teacher prompt:* Demonstrate one card at each station before letting children rotate. 'At MATCH, you pick a card and find the bowl that has THAT MANY. At ORDER, the cards have to go SMALLEST to BIGGEST on the clothesline. At TRACE, you put your pencil on the dot and follow the arrow.'

**Step 2.** Rotate every 6 minutes. After each rotation, ring a small bell or clap a 1-2-3 rhythm and have children move clockwise to the next station with their work-in-progress (tracing strip travels with them; cards stay at the station for the next group). Circulate during ORDER especially — that's where the tricky teens trip children up. When a child clips 13 between 30 and 14 (or anywhere wrong in the teen run), pause and ask: 'Look at the number BEFORE 13. What is it? And the number AFTER 13? Which is bigger?'

> *Teacher prompt:* At MATCH: 'Show me — does this card match this bowl? Let us count the bears together. One … two … three … four … five … six … seven. And the card says?' At TRACE: 'Start at the dot. Follow the arrow. The teen numbers always start with a ONE. So 13 is ONE then THREE.'

**Step 3.** Final 4 minutes: pull the whole class back to the long carpet clothesline. The MATCH and ORDER stations have produced a wall of evidence. Now play 'Tricky Teens Tap'. Hold up a number — say it aloud (e.g., 'fourteen!') — and children tap their head if they hear a teen, tap their feet if they hear something else. Then point to the teen poster and have children chorus the digits: 'one-four', 'one-five', 'one-six' …. This rapid review locks in the visual-auditory link.

> *Teacher prompt:* Vary your call: 'fifteen!', 'fifty!', 'twelve!', 'twenty!', 'eighteen!', 'eighty!'. Watch which children tap correctly without hesitation — those are secure on A6.3 for teens. Children who freeze on 'fifty vs. fifteen' need the small-group recovery from the expanded walkthrough.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Emerging children stay in the 0-10 range at all three stations. Their MATCH bowls have 1-10 quantities only. Their ORDER cards are 0-10. Their TRACE strip is 0-10. They get the teen exposure at the whole-class moment but are not assessed on writing teens this lesson — that's a Lesson 5 stretch.
- **For extending learners:** Extending children stretch beyond 20: at MATCH they can match any card up to 25 (extra cards added to the tray); at ORDER they can place 0-25 in sequence; at TRACE they can write 21, 22, 23 unsupported on the back of their strip. Some can also draw the 'tens-frame plus extras' picture under each teen on the poster.
- **For ELL / IEP:** Pre-teach the teen-name pattern: 'thirteen sounds like THREE-teen because it has a 3 in it. Fourteen sounds like FOUR-teen because it has a 4.' Use the linking-cube tens-stick (a tower of 10) plus loose cubes as a tactile model: 'one ten and three more is THIRTEEN'. Allow tracing in coloured marker so the digit shape stands out. Pair with a same-language partner if possible.
- **For movement learners:** These children get the role of 'card runner' — they carry stacks of new cards between stations and clip them on the long clothesline at the end. They also lead the 'Tricky Teens Tap' physical response. Bonus regulation: each move between stations is a 'walking-on-tippy-toes' transition.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Three signals: (a) at MATCH, does the child match the card to a quantity by counting one-to-one, or do they recognise the numeral and just KNOW the quantity (the latter is secure A6.3)? (b) at ORDER, can the child sequence 0-10 cleanly, AND do they place 11-20 in correct order without flipping a teen? (c) at TRACE, does the child form digits left-to-right and top-to-bottom (correct stroke order), or are they 'drawing the shape' without direction? Stroke-order matters because reversed digits (Ɛ for 3, ƌ for 6) are common at K and need correction now.
- **How to record:** Class formative checklist with rows for each child and three columns: 'matches numeral to quantity 0-10', 'orders teens correctly', 'forms digits with correct stroke direction'. Plus a comments column for reversed digits or specific teens that confused (e.g., '13/14 swapped' or 'reverses 6'). Keep the tracing strips — they are evidence for the per-child portfolio.
- **Tracker columns:** matches_numeral_to_quantity_0_10, orders_teens_correctly, forms_digits_with_correct_stroke

### Consolidation (10 min) — Counting Crew Conversation — Tricky Teens

**Discussion prompts:**
- Mae just asked us — when she writes 13, what TWO digits does she use? Show me with your fingers.
- Theo is mixing up 14 and 40. How can we help him tell them apart? Look at the teen poster.
- If I say a number out loud and you have to find it on the clothesline, what is the FIRST clue you look for? (Answer we want: 'the first digit — is it a 1?').

**Exit routine:** Each child holds up a number card from their pocket as they leave and reads it aloud — first the digits ('one … three'), then the name ('thirteen'). Teacher tallies on the anchor chart who said it cleanly, who said the wrong name (e.g., '13' read as 'thirty'), and who needed the digit-by-digit prompt.

### Assessment (formative)

- **Evidence collected:** Per-child formative checklist with three indicators (matches numeral to quantity, orders teens correctly, forms digits with correct stroke). Tracing strips kept as portfolio evidence — the teen numbers are circled if formed correctly, marked with a sticky-flag if reversed or skipped. Photo of the long clothesline with all 0-20 cards in order at the end of class. Exit-routine tally showing which children read teens aloud cleanly versus needed prompts.
- **Tracker:** `L3_formative_tracker`
- **Rationale:** A6.3 'identify and write numbers to 20' is the most concrete and observable expectation in the unit — every child either writes the digit or they don't. The formative tier is appropriate because Lesson 5 will revisit number identification (children read numerals when comparing groups), and the diagnostic-from-Lesson-1 plus the formative-from-here together feed the Mid-Unit Formative Reflection. Children flagged for digit reversals get a one-on-one tracing minute in the morning meeting before Lesson 4.

### Expanded walkthrough — Repairing 'Thirty / Thirteen' confusion — using the digit count, not the sound

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* The hardest piece of A6.3 at K is the teen / decade confusion. 'Thirteen' and 'thirty' sound nearly identical to a five-year-old's ear, and both contain a 3, so the auditory channel alone cannot disambiguate them. The repair must be VISUAL: thirteen has TWO digits (1 and 3), thirty has TWO digits (3 and 0), but the order is different and the FIRST DIGIT is the clue. Step 2 (circulate during ORDER) is where this confusion shows up — a child clips 30 in the teen run, or pulls a teen card and reads it as a decade. The script below shows exactly what to say, what to point at, and why the linking-cube tens-stick is the recovery tool.

**Setup:** ORDER station has a clothesline at child height with pegs. The child has a 0-20 deck and is clipping cards in sequence. They have just clipped 12 then put 30 in the next slot (instead of 13). On a small table beside the clothesline sits a tens-stick (10 linking cubes pre-snapped together) plus a small pile of loose cubes. Teacher carries the formative checklist clipboard.

**Teacher script:**

> Crouch beside the child. Don't say 'that's wrong' — instead, say: 'Walk me through your line. What did you peg first?' They'll likely say 'zero, then one, then two …'. Let them go all the way to 12. 'And then?' They'll point at the 30 card. 'And the next one?' They'll probably hesitate. Now hold up the tens-stick of 10 linked cubes. 'This is TEN. One stick of ten cubes.' Add three loose cubes beside it. 'TEN and three more. Count them with me — ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen.' Pause. 'Thirteen is a TEEN — it starts with a TEN-stick and has THREE extras. Now look at the card you pegged.' Hold up the 30 card. 'The 30 card has a 3 first, then a 0. Three first means THREE tens-sticks. Let me show you.' Pull out three tens-sticks. 'THREE tens-sticks is THIRTY — way more than thirteen.' Set them side by side: thirteen (one tens-stick + 3 cubes) vs. thirty (three tens-sticks). The visual difference is enormous and unmistakable. 'Which one belongs after twelve on our clothesline — thirteen or thirty?' Wait. They'll usually pick thirteen now. 'Yes! And how do we WRITE thirteen?' Hold up the 13 card. 'One, then three. One ten-stick and three more.' Have them swap the 30 card for the 13 card. Now point at 14, 15, 16 still in the deck. 'Each of these starts with a ONE because each is a TEN-stick plus more. Pegging 14 next?' They peg 14. Continue together to 19. Tick the 'orders teens correctly' column with a small note: 'needed tens-stick re-show'. That's a flag for tomorrow's morning meeting — give them a 90-second tens-stick refresh before Lesson 4 starts.

**Expected student responses:**
- (Confused) Pegs 30 after 12, says 'thirteen' but reads the card as thirty when prompted — auditory/visual mismatch unresolved. Most common K starting point.
- (Mid) After the tens-stick demo, swaps the card and continues correctly through 13 and 14 but stalls again at 15 — needs you to show the tens-stick + 5 cubes once more before the pattern locks.
- (Strong) After ONE tens-stick demo, says 'oh — every teen is one ten-stick plus extras' and pegs all the way to 19 unsupported.
- (Stretched) Volunteers: 'And twenty is TWO ten-sticks!' — generalises the tens-stick model beyond the teens. Note this child for the Lesson-5 stretch task on composing 20.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Thirty and thirteen are the same number. (Auditory similarity dominates; the child cannot yet use the written digits to disambiguate. Most common at K.)
- Teens come AFTER decades. (Some children peg 30, 40, 50 between 10 and 20 because they sound 'more like teens than ones do'. The tens-stick demo is the only durable repair.)
- The number 12 says 'one-two' so the next number must say 'one-three' which is 13 ✓ — but then 14 must say 'one-four' which they correctly write, BUT they READ it as 'fourteen' or 'forty' interchangeably. (Reading and writing diverge at this stage.)

**Recovery moves:**
- Always show the tens-stick. Words like 'teen' and 'ty' don't carry meaning at K — but a ten-stick of cubes physically demonstrates the difference between ONE ten-stick (teen) and many ten-sticks (decade).
- Point to the FIRST digit every time. 'Is the first digit a 1? Then it is a teen. If the first digit is 3, 4, 5, it is a decade.' This decoding rule is more durable than name-to-name comparison.
- Pair the visual with chanted speech. After fixing one teen, have the child chant '11, 12, 13, 14, 15' while pointing at each card — the rhythm + visual locks the sequence.

## Lesson 4: How Many Without Counting

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Kindergarten |
| Strand | A. Foundations of Language and Mathematics — Number Sense and Operations |
| Duration | 40 minutes |
| Primary expectations | A6.4 |
| Secondary expectations | A6.2 |

**Big idea:** We can sometimes know HOW MANY just by LOOKING — we don't have to count one by one. This trick is called subitizing, and it works for small groups of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Past five, our eyes need help and we have to count.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to subitize — to look at a small group of dots, paw prints, or cubes and say HOW MANY straight away, without counting one by one.

**Student learning goal:** I can look at a small group and say how many without counting.

**Success criteria:**
- I can look at a flash of 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 dots and say the number out loud before the teacher hides the card.
- I can tell my partner whether the dots were in a 'dice pattern' or a 'random pattern'.
- I can sort dot cards into 'easy to subitize' (1-5) and 'too many — I need to count' (6+) piles.

**Vocabulary introduced:** subitize, flash, pattern
**Vocabulary reinforced:** count, number, more, less, forward, backward
**Manipulatives used:** M3_dot_cards, M2_ten_frames, M1_counting_cubes

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Subitizing Dot Cards (M3) — 1-5 dots, both standard dice patterns and random arrangements, plus a few 'too many' cards (6, 7, 8 dots) to seed the sort
- A pile of paw-print cards (1-5 paw prints, modelled on Buddy the puppy's footprint) per partner pair
- Laminated ten-frames (M2), one per partner pair
- Linking cubes (5-cube sets, two colours per pair)
- A small pocket chart at the carpet for whole-group flashes
- A large 'subitize / count' sorting mat with two big circles labelled 'I see it!' (subitize) and 'I count it' (count)
- Anchor chart paper labelled 'Subitize Without Counting — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5'

### Minds On (10 min) — Buddy's Paw-Print Flash

**Hook:** Buddy the puppy is back, and he has a NEW trick. Today he is going to show us how to spot HOW MANY paw prints he leaves WITHOUT counting them. This trick is called subitizing, and only super-fast eyes can do it.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up Buddy and a small stack of paw-print cards. 'Friends — Buddy walked through a puddle and left paw prints. I am going to FLASH a card very fast, and your job is to say how many paw prints you saw without counting one by one.'
> 
>  Hold up a 3-paw-print card. 'Watch.' Show for one second, hide it. 'How many?' Children chorus 'three'. 'Yes — and did you have time to say one … two … three? No! Your eyes JUST KNEW. That is called subitizing.' Repeat with 1, 2, 4, 5. Then flash a card with 7 paw prints — show for the same one second. Hide. 'How many?' The room will go quiet, or guesses will scatter. 'See — your eyes COULD NOT just know. Seven was too many. We have to COUNT. Today our job is to find out which numbers our eyes can JUST KNOW, and which numbers we have to count.'

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a half-circle facing the teacher and the pocket chart.
- Watch each card flash silently — no calling out before the card is hidden.
- Chorus the number on the count of three after each card hides.
- Notice when the room goes quiet (the 'too many' cards) versus when everyone shouts together (the 1-5 cards).

### Action (20 min) — Subitize, Build, Sort — Partner Centres

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_centres`

**Step 1.** Whole-group warm-up flash with the dot cards in pocket-chart. Flash 10 cards in a row, each for one second only. Mix in dice patterns (the dot pattern children know from board games) and random patterns. After every five cards, ask 'show me with your fingers' before saying the number aloud. This warms eyes and forces silent subitizing before the chorus, so you can SEE who has the answer ready and who copies the chorus.

> *Teacher prompt:* After each flash, say: 'Hold up your fingers — don't say it yet. … Now whisper to your partner. … Now say it together: THREE!' For dice-pattern cards add: 'The dots look like a dice — that helps your eyes know fast.' For random patterns add: 'The dots are scattered — but your eyes can still see TWO and ONE — that's three.'

**Step 2.** Move to partner centres in two rotations of 7 minutes each. Centre BUILD: each pair has a ten-frame, two colours of linking cubes, and a stack of dot cards. One partner flashes a card; the other partner builds the matching number on the ten-frame using cubes. Then they swap roles. Centre SORT: each pair has the big sorting mat ('I see it!' / 'I count it') and a mixed deck of dot cards (1-8 dots). They sort each card based on whether the quantity is small enough to subitize (1-5) or too many (6+). When they disagree, they hold the card up and discuss before placing.

> *Teacher prompt:* At BUILD: 'Show me on the ten-frame — if the card has 4 dots, fill 4 cells, leave 6 empty. Same number, different way of seeing it.' At SORT: 'If you had to COUNT to know, it goes in the I COUNT IT pile. If your eyes JUST KNEW, it goes in I SEE IT.'

**Step 3.** Whole class returns to the carpet for the final 4-minute 'subitize-and-tell' game. Teacher flashes one card; called-on child not only says the number but also tells HOW they saw it — e.g., 'I saw two dots and two more dots. That's four.' This narration step is critical — it is what separates 'rote-knew the answer' from 'visually decomposed the group'. Children who can narrate are securely subitizing on A6.4.

> *Teacher prompt:* After a child gives their number, ask: 'How did your eyes see it? Did you see one group, or two little groups added together?' Affirm both — children who see 4 as 'two and two' are already pre-composing for Lesson 5. Children who see 4 as 'just four' are also correct — that's the dice-pattern recognition working.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Emerging children stay in the 1-3 range during the warm-up flash and the BUILD centre. The teacher adjusts their dot-card deck to remove 4-and-5 cards. At SORT, they sort only 1-5 cards (no 6+ confusers). They are assessed on subitizing 1-3 only — anything above is a stretch.
- **For extending learners:** Extending children stretch to 6 and 7 — they are introduced to 'partial subitizing' (seeing 6 as 'two threes' or '5 and 1'). At BUILD, they predict the ten-frame layout BEFORE flipping the card ('I think 4 will be two on top, two on bottom'). At SORT, they create a third pile: 'I subitize by GROUPING' — for cards where they used a chunk strategy.
- **For ELL / IEP:** Pre-teach 'subitize' with the gesture of rapidly opening fingers (one quick burst) versus the gesture of counting (one finger raised at a time). Use the dice patterns first — many children know dice from board games and can read 1-6 instantly even if the WORD 'subitize' is new. Provide a partner who narrates each flash aloud in the home language during the warm-up. Allow gestures-only response in the SORT centre.
- **For movement learners:** These children get the role of 'flash master' during the warm-up — they hold and flash the cards for the whole group while the teacher coaches. At BUILD they get to physically place cubes on the ten-frame (active hands). They can also do a 'jump-and-shout' version: flash a card, child jumps the number of times AND shouts the number. The body movement strengthens the number-to-quantity link.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** Three signals: (a) on the warm-up flash, does the child have the answer ready BEFORE the chorus (seen by finger-up speed), or do they wait and copy peers? (b) at BUILD, does the child place the right number of cubes on the FIRST attempt, or do they re-count and adjust? (c) at the subitize-and-tell narration, can the child tell HOW their eyes saw the number ('I saw three and one — that's four'), or do they just say the number without explanation? Children who narrate AND build correctly on the first try are securely subitizing on A6.4.
- **How to record:** Per-child tally sheet: out of 5 quick flashes (1, 2, 3, 4, 5 dots, in random order), how many did the child get right BEFORE the chorus prompt. A score of 4-5 = secure A6.4. A score of 2-3 = developing. A score of 0-1 = needs revisit. Plus a comments column for children who use a 'two-and-two' chunk strategy (an early-composing signal worth flagging for Lesson 5).
- **Tracker columns:** subitize_score_out_of_5, narrates_how_seen, builds_correct_on_first_try

### Consolidation (10 min) — Counting Crew Conversation — Eyes That Know

**Discussion prompts:**
- Buddy wants to know — what is the BIGGEST number our eyes can just KNOW without counting? (Answer: five.)
- Mae just asked us — when she sees a dice and rolls a 4, does she count the dots or just KNOW? Why?
- Show me with your hands: ONE big group of 6 versus TWO little groups of 3 — which is easier to subitize? Why?

**Exit routine:** On the way out, the teacher flashes ONE final card per child (1-5 dots). Each child whispers the number into the teacher's ear. Teacher tallies: green sticker for instant correct, yellow for hesitated-then-correct, red for needed counting. The class wall has a 'Subitize Wall of Fame' chart that fills with green stickers as the unit progresses.

### Assessment (formative)

- **Evidence collected:** Per-child subitize-out-of-5 score from the warm-up flash. Photo of each pair's BUILD ten-frames showing a sequence of correctly built quantities. Photo of each pair's SORT mat showing 1-5 in 'I see it' and 6-8 in 'I count it'. Exit-routine sticker tally (green/yellow/red) feeding the 'Subitize Wall of Fame' chart. A short audio note (teacher voice memo) of two or three children's narration during subitize-and-tell, capturing the 'two and two makes four' chunk-strategy signal.
- **Tracker:** `L4_formative_tracker`

### Expanded walkthrough — When the Child Counts Instead of Subitizing — re-tuning the eye to the dice pattern

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* The central A6.4 trap at K is the child who CAN see a 4-dot card and CAN say 'four' — but only after touching each dot one by one with their finger and counting subvocally. Counting-then-saying is NOT subitizing. The window the card is exposed for must be short enough to prevent finger-counting (one second is the field-tested upper bound). The repair is two-fold: (a) reduce the flash time, and (b) give the child the dice-pattern shortcut so their eye has a CHUNK to grab onto instead of dots-to-count. This walkthrough covers exactly what to do when a pair's BUILD centre shows correct cube-counts but you noticed the child was finger-counting on the warm-up.

**Setup:** Warm-up flash has just ended. You're moving children to the BUILD centre. One specific child (call her Alex) got 5/5 on the warm-up flash — but you watched her finger-tap each dot card with her index finger before answering. Her answers were correct, but they were SLOW (~3-4 seconds each) and visually mediated by counting. Now Alex is at BUILD with her partner. You stop by their station 90 seconds in.

**Teacher script:**

> Crouch beside Alex. Pick up a dice-pattern 4-card (the standard dice-4 layout with dots at the four corners). Don't flash it — show it openly. 'Alex, look at this card. Don't tell me the number. Look at the SHAPE.' Pause. 'How many corners does this dot-pattern have?' She'll say 'four' (corners is a known word). 'Yes — and the dots are at every corner. So this is the dice-4 pattern. Anytime you see this shape — boom — your eyes know it is four. You don't have to count.' Now flip the card face down. Flip it back up for one second. Hide it. 'How many?' She says 'four'. 'Did you count?' She'll think — usually says no. 'GREAT — your eyes JUST KNEW because you remembered the shape.' Repeat with the dice-3 pattern (a diagonal of 3 dots). 'This is the dice-3 — a slanted line. Three dots on a slant.' Flash, hide. 'How many?' Then dice-2 (two dots opposite corners). Then dice-5 (four corners + a centre dot). Don't do dice-1 (it's trivial). After all four, mix the deck and re-flash quickly — 0.7 to 1.0 second per card. Watch her finger. If it stays still, she is now subitizing. Tick the 'subitize_score' column with a note: 'switched from count-on-fingers to instant — used dice patterns'. If her finger STILL twitches toward the card, drop to a 0.5-second flash for two more rounds. Past that, she is genuinely on the subitize-by-counting boundary and needs the random-pattern recovery from the next planning cycle.

**Expected student responses:**
- (Confused) Finger-taps each dot during the warm-up flash, gives correct answers slowly. The card might as well not have been hidden.
- (Mid) After the dice-pattern teach-in, can subitize dice-3, dice-4, dice-5 instantly but slips back into counting on random patterns. Normal — random subitizing develops after dice subitizing.
- (Strong) Recognises dice-4 as four AND when shown a random-4 (e.g., a Y-shape of dots) says 'four — three plus one'. Has internalised both pattern-based AND chunk-based subitizing.
- (Stretched) Volunteers without prompting: 'I bet 6 is just two threes' or 'a dice-5 plus one is six'. Generalising the chunk strategy beyond five — flag for Lesson 5 stretch.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Subitizing means counting fast. (Child believes the only difference between subitizing and counting is speed. The repair is to make the flash so fast that finger-counting is impossible — then the child realises their EYES did the work, not their finger.)
- Random patterns can't be subitized. (Some children can subitize dice patterns but freeze on random arrangements. The repair is the chunk strategy: 'I see two and one — that's three.' Random subitizing is composed, not perceptual, but counts as A6.4.)
- Past five is impossible. (Children correctly observe their eyes can't subitize 7 — but some over-generalise to 'past five there is no shortcut'. In Lesson 5 they will learn the 'I see 5 and 2 more' decomposition. Don't worry about this misconception today; it self-resolves.)

**Recovery moves:**
- Drop the flash time. If the child finger-counts, they are getting too much exposure. Reduce from 1 second to 0.7 to 0.5 — past that, the eye HAS to do the work.
- Teach the dice-pattern shortcut. Most children know 1-6 dice patterns from board games — leverage that recognition before introducing random arrangements.
- Have them BUILD the pattern themselves on the ten-frame. The BUILD centre IS the recovery move for children who have weak perceptual anchors — placing four cubes in a 2-and-2 arrangement physically teaches the chunk.

## Lesson 5: Building Numbers

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Grade | Kindergarten |
| Strand | A. Foundations of Language and Mathematics — Number Sense and Operations |
| Duration | 45 minutes |
| Primary expectations | A6.5, A6.6 |
| Secondary expectations | A6.1, A6.2, A6.3, A6.4 |

**Big idea:** We can put two smaller groups together to make a bigger group (compose), or break a bigger group into two smaller ones (decompose). When we have two groups, we can also tell whether one is MORE, LESS, or EQUAL to the other.

**Learning intention (teacher):** We are learning to build a target number lots of different ways using cubes, then to compare two groups and decide whether the first has MORE than, LESS than, or is EQUAL to the second.

**Student learning goal:** I can build a number lots of different ways and compare two groups using more, less, or equal.

**Success criteria:**
- I can build the number 7 using two different combinations of cubes (for example 4 and 3, or 5 and 2).
- I can decompose a group of 7 cubes into two smaller groups in two different ways.
- I can compare two plates of objects and circle MORE / LESS / EQUAL with a reason ('this plate has 6, this plate has 4 — 6 is more than 4').
- I can use the words MORE, LESS, and EQUAL correctly when I talk about my partner's plate.

**Vocabulary introduced:** compose, decompose, equal, comparison
**Vocabulary reinforced:** count, number, more, less, subitize, forward, backward
**Manipulatives used:** M1_counting_cubes, M2_ten_frames, M6_compare_strip, M5_counting_objects_set, M4_number_cards

**Lesson-specific materials:**
- Two-colour linking cubes (red and yellow), at least 10 of each colour per child
- Laminated ten-frames (M2), one per child
- Comparison strip (M6) — two parallel sentence-strip rows for visual side-by-side comparing — one per partner pair
- A small basket per pair with two paper plates labelled PLATE A and PLATE B
- Tim Hortons-style timbit counters or paper-circle 'cookies' (~30 per pair) for the cookie-plate comparison
- Number cards 0-10 (M4), one set per pair, used to randomly draw target compose numbers
- Three big circle cards labelled MORE, LESS, EQUAL, for the whole-class anchor
- Anchor chart paper labelled 'Building 7' on the left and 'Comparing Plates' on the right with MORE / LESS / EQUAL columns

### Minds On (12 min) — Buddy's Bone Plates

**Hook:** Buddy is hungry, and he has TWO plates of bones to choose from. He wants the plate with MORE bones — but he doesn't know which one that is. The Counting Crew chefs need our help to figure out which plate has more, which has less, and whether they might be EQUAL.

**Teacher script:**

> Bring children to the carpet. Hold up Buddy and two paper plates with paper-bone cutouts taped on top. 'Friends, Plate A has 6 bones. Plate B has 4 bones.' Hold them up side by side. 'Buddy wants the plate with MORE. Which one should he eat from?'
> 
>  Children will likely point at A. 'How do you KNOW it has more?' Take responses. Affirm: 'Yes — six is more than four. We can also say four is LESS than six.' Lay out three big circle cards on the carpet: MORE / LESS / EQUAL. 'These are our three comparing words. We use ALL THREE today.' Now hold up two plates with three bones EACH. 'Hmm — these look the same. Three and three. We say they are EQUAL — no more, no less, exactly the same amount.' Then introduce composing: hold up a number card 7. 'Today we also build the same number lots of different ways. Watch — five red cubes and two yellow cubes is SEVEN.' Snap them together and count. 'But four red and three yellow is ALSO seven.' Snap them. 'Same SEVEN — different way of building it. That's called composing.'

**Student actions:**
- Sit in a half-circle facing the two paper plates.
- Help compare 6 vs. 4 bones using the words MORE and LESS.
- Help compare 3 vs. 3 bones and discover the new word EQUAL.
- Watch the teacher build 7 two different ways with red and yellow cubes.
- Notice that the same number can be built more than one way.

### Action (23 min) — Building 7 Lots of Ways, Then Comparing Plates

*Structure:* `whole_group_then_partner`

**Step 1.** Whole-group compose demo with target number 7. The class chants 'we are building seven, in lots of ways'. The teacher invites four children up one at a time. Each child uses red and yellow cubes to build 7 a different way and snaps the tower onto a long horizontal track on the easel. Likely contributions: 0+7, 1+6, 2+5, 3+4. Class chants the matching addition fact each time ('zero and seven is seven'). Teacher records each composition on the 'Building 7' anchor column with a coloured icon: red square + yellow square + total.

> *Teacher prompt:* Say: 'Friends, our target is SEVEN. Build it any way you like with red and yellow cubes. Take any number of red — even zero — and add yellows until you have seven.' Watch for the child who builds 7+0 and lift it up: 'Look — Carla used zero red and seven yellow. That STILL makes seven. Zero is a real number too.'

**Step 2.** Partner compose station. Each pair has a number card deck (0-10), two-colour cubes, and a ten-frame each. Partner A draws a card (e.g., 8). Both partners build that number two different ways on their own ten-frames. Then they swap towers and check each other's count. Repeat with three or four target numbers. The teacher walks the room with the formative checklist; children who build TWO valid combinations for each number are securely composing on A6.5.

> *Teacher prompt:* At the pair: 'Show me your ten-frame. You drew 8. Build it one way — got it? Now build it a DIFFERENT way. The total is still 8 but the colours are different.' For struggling children: 'Take 8 cubes total. Pull off 2 cubes — how many are left? 6. So 2 and 6 makes 8. Try another split.'

**Step 3.** Switch to compare station with cookie plates and the comparison strip. Each pair gets two paper plates and ~30 paper cookies. Partner A makes a plate (any number 1-10). Partner B makes a different plate. They line the cookies up on the comparison strip — Plate A's row above, Plate B's row below — so the visual side-by-side is unmistakable. Then they pick the right circle card: MORE / LESS / EQUAL. Each pair completes at least four rounds, switching who makes which plate.

> *Teacher prompt:* At the pair: 'Line them up so each cookie on top has a buddy below — what's left over tells you the answer. If the top row has extras, top is MORE. If the bottom row has extras, top is LESS. If everyone has a buddy, they are EQUAL.' Listen for children using the words spontaneously and tick the formative column.

**Step 4.** Whole-class debrief. Pull pairs back to the carpet with one of their cookie comparisons (their favourite — most surprising or trickiest). Each pair holds up their two plates and the room calls out 'MORE!', 'LESS!', or 'EQUAL!' before the pair confirms with the comparison strip. This rapid review locks in the three-word vocabulary as a class identity ('we are the people who use these three words').

> *Teacher prompt:* Say: 'Pair-A, hold up your plates. Class — eyes only, no counting out loud — is plate A more, less, or equal to plate B?' If the class is split, GREAT — line them up on the comparison strip together. The disagreement is the teaching moment.

#### Differentiation

- **For emerging learners:** Emerging children stay with target numbers 3-5 at the compose station. Their cube count is reduced (5 cubes total) so the decomposition is fewer and clearer. At compare, they only use plates with 1-5 cookies each (which they can subitize from Lesson 4). They are assessed on building one decomposition correctly (not two) and on choosing the right comparison word for plates that differ by at least 2.
- **For extending learners:** Extending children stretch to target numbers 8-10 (or 12, 15 if confident). At compose they look for ALL combinations for a target (e.g., 7 = 0+7, 1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1, 7+0 — the systematic ladder). At compare they use plates with quantities up to 20 and explain HOW MUCH MORE (not just 'more' but '6 more than 4 is 2 more, because 6 take away 4 is 2'). Some can predict before lining up: 'I think A has more because it has both rows of the ten-frame and B only has one row'.
- **For ELL / IEP:** Pre-teach the three comparison words with hand gestures: MORE (one hand high, one hand low), LESS (the reverse), EQUAL (two hands at the same height). Children echo the gesture every time the word is spoken. At compose, provide pre-snapped towers (e.g., a 5-tower, a 3-tower) so the child only has to choose which two go together to make the target, not build from scratch. At compare, allow drawing/pointing instead of saying the word. Pair with a strong-language partner who narrates aloud during the round.
- **For movement learners:** These children take the role of 'plate carrier' between pairs — they walk plates from one pair to the next during the debrief. At compose they get to physically build on a large floor ten-frame (using whole linking-cube towers as 'cubes'). They can also do a 'compose-relay': one partner shouts a target, the other partner runs to the cube tub, grabs that many cubes, and races back to build it on the ten-frame. The body movement strengthens both the count and the composition.

#### Formative check

- **What to look for:** FOUR signals because this lesson covers two expectations. (a) at compose, can the child build the target number with TWO different combinations on the first try? (b) at compose, do they use both colours strategically (red + yellow with a clear visual split), or do they grab any cubes? (c) at compare, do they line objects up on the comparison strip BEFORE picking a circle card, or do they pick a card visually without checking? (d) do they use the right word — MORE / LESS / EQUAL — in spontaneous talk with their partner, not just when prompted? Children who hit all four are securely composing AND comparing on A6.5 + A6.6.
- **How to record:** Per-child summative checklist with four columns: 'composes target two ways', 'uses two-colour split', 'lines up before comparing', 'uses correct comparison word'. Plus a portfolio sample: take a photo of each child's ten-frame at the moment they show two different decompositions. Plus a tally column for each comparison word the child used during the partner round (count how many times MORE, LESS, EQUAL came up).
- **Tracker columns:** composes_target_two_ways, uses_two_colour_split, lines_up_before_comparing, uses_correct_comparison_word

### Consolidation (10 min) — Counting Crew Conversation — Same Number, Many Ways

**Discussion prompts:**
- Mae just asked us — when she has 4 cookies and Theo has 4 cookies, what word do we use? (Answer: equal.)
- Theo wants to know — if you build 6 with three reds and three yellows, can your partner build 6 a different way? Show me with your fingers — how many ways could 6 be?
- Buddy got two plates: one with 5 bones, one with 3. Which has more? How can you prove it WITHOUT counting one by one? (Look for: subitize each plate, then compare; or line them up and look for extras.)
- When is it useful in real life to know if you have MORE or LESS? Think about lunch, recess, sharing. Tell your partner one example.

**Exit routine:** Each child takes one number card 1-10 from the basket as they walk out and shows the teacher TWO ways to build that number with their fingers (e.g., for 5: shows 2+3, then 4+1). Teacher tallies on the 'Building Wall' chart who showed two ways instantly, who needed a prompt, and who could only show one way. The wall fills with names so the children see their progress as the unit closes.

### Expanded walkthrough — When the Child Builds Two Compositions That Are Actually the Same — teaching the difference between 'flipped' and 'different'

*Why this lesson warrants extra scripting:* The subtle A6.5 trap at K is the child who builds 7 as '4 red + 3 yellow' and then proudly shows their second composition as '3 red + 4 yellow' — same numbers, opposite colours. To an adult, those LOOK like two compositions; to the curriculum, they ARE two compositions (commutativity); but to a kindergartner, the question 'are these different ways to make 7?' is unsettled. The teacher's job is to AFFIRM both as legitimate while ALSO pushing the child to find a TRULY different split (e.g., 5+2 or 6+1) so the decomposition lattice grows. Step 2 (partner compose station) is where this comes up. The script below covers exactly what to say and why colour-flipping counts but should not be the ONLY answer.

**Setup:** Compose station has a target number card 7 in front of the pair. The child has built two ten-frames: one shows 4 red + 3 yellow, the other shows 3 red + 4 yellow. The child is grinning, proud — they think they are done. Teacher walks up with the formative checklist clipboard.

**Teacher script:**

> Crouch beside the child. 'Wow, look at your two builds. Tell me about them.' Let them narrate. 'Four red and three yellow is seven. And three red and four yellow is seven.' Affirm GENEROUSLY first: 'YES — both are seven. The colour doesn't change the total. That is a real grown-up math idea — three plus four and four plus three are equal.' Pause. 'But I'm wondering — could you build seven WITHOUT using a 4 and a 3 at all? Could there be a way to make seven that has different NUMBERS of cubes, not just different colours?' Wait. The child will think. Sometimes they'll try 5+2 or 6+1; sometimes they'll need a hint. Hint: 'What if you only had ONE red cube? How many yellow would you need?' Wait. They count up: '6 yellow'. 'YES — 1 and 6 makes 7. That is a NEW way, with NEW numbers. Build it on a third ten-frame.' If you only have two ten-frames, sweep one of the colour-flipped builds away and rebuild as 1+6 in its place. 'Now you have three different ways: 4 and 3, 3 and 4, 1 and 6. The first two are colour-flips of each other — they're the SAME pair of numbers. The third is a DIFFERENT pair of numbers. Both kinds count as decomposing — but a really strong mathematician finds different number PAIRS, not just different colour orders.' Tick the 'composes target two ways' column. If the child gets to a different number pair on their own (e.g., 1+6 OR 5+2), tick TWO levels. If they only have colour flips, tick one level and note 'colour-flip only — re-prompt at decompose station next round'. By Lesson-5 standard, a child who can find at least one different number pair has mastered A6.5. A child who finds three different number pairs (e.g., 1+6, 2+5, 3+4) has surpassed it.

**Expected student responses:**
- (Confused) Builds 4+3 and 3+4 and stops. Treats colour-flip as the second way. Most common K starting point.
- (Mid) After the prompt 'what if you only had 1 red?', builds 1+6 successfully but cannot find a third pair on their own without further prompts.
- (Strong) Without prompting, finds 4+3, then 5+2, then 6+1, then 7+0. The decomposition lattice is forming.
- (Stretched) Volunteers an EVEN-numbered observation: 'I bet 8 has different ones — 4 and 4 is the same colour split though, that's weird.' Generalises the lattice and notices the 'middle' decomposition. Flag as ready for Grade-1 stretch.

**Common misconceptions:**
- Colour flips are 'different ways'. (Most common at K. Pedagogically defensible — commutativity IS a valid second representation — but the child needs to also find pairs with different numbers, not just different colours.)
- There's only one 'right' way to make 7. (Some children look for THE answer, like a sum problem. Affirm explicitly: there are EIGHT correct decompositions of 7 — 0+7 through 7+0 — and a kindergartner who finds even three is excellent.)
- Bigger numbers have fewer decompositions. (Children sometimes think 9 is 'harder' so it must be more restrictive. The opposite is true — 9 has more decompositions than 5. The wall chart visually corrects this if you keep adding new decompositions to the 'Building Wall'.)

**Recovery moves:**
- Always affirm the colour-flip first. The child has done real math. THEN push for a different number pair so the decomposition lattice grows. Affirm-then-extend prevents the child from feeling 'wrong' for offering commutativity.
- Use the '1-and-rest' prompt. 'What if you only had 1 red?' is a near-universal scaffolding move that gets children to a new decomposition without having to remember the full strategy.
- Build the decomposition wall. As pairs find new decompositions, add them to the anchor chart. Children who have not yet found 5+2 will see another pair show 5+2 and copy it — peer learning is the fastest path through the decomposition lattice.

### Overall prep notes

Total prep time across all 10 assets is approximately 205 minutes (~3.5 hours) for a class of 20 — heavier than a typical unit because the counting-cube Sorting Mat and the Counting Path floor mat are both per-child / per-class scale. Default print spec is BLACK AND WHITE for every asset so any classroom printer can produce the unit. Recommended approach: front-load the bulk prep across two evenings the week before the unit launches — Evening 1 print+laminate everything, Evening 2 cut and store. Highest-yield prep priorities if time is tight: M1 Cube Sorting Mats (used every day), M3 Buddy dot cards (the centrepiece of Lesson 4), and the three character puppets (Mae, Theo, Buddy — referenced by name in every lesson's teacher script). M7 Counting Path is the most labour-intensive single asset; if prep time is critical, skip lamination and tape-protect the tiles instead, or use a chalk number line drawn directly on the gym floor as an emergency substitute. Storage: a single labelled bin on the math shelf with the puppets clipped to one side in a folder, the laminated mats stacked on the other, and the cube/object bags lined along the bottom. Re-using the unit next year takes zero re-prep if every asset was laminated this year.

### Building Numbers — Summative Administration Guide

*Ties the Lesson 5 worksheet, the lesson plan's three phases (Compose-Decompose-Compare), and the rubric together. Tells the teacher exactly what evidence to look for during each phase and which expectations each phase's evidence maps to. Designed so the teacher can score every child on all six expectations from a single lesson plus the worksheet artefact.*

#### Administration steps

1. Before the lesson: photocopy one Lesson 5 worksheet ('Building Numbers') per child. Print one rubric per child or maintain a single class rubric on a clipboard with student-name rows pre-filled. Have the Junior Counting Crew certificates printed and ready for the closing ceremony — do not show them to children before they have earned them. Prepare the comparison-strip mats and a basket of mixed counting cubes per pair.
2. Phase 1 (Compose, 12 min): Children build the target number 7 in two different ways using counting cubes, then record each split on the worksheet's two-colour cube row. This produces visible evidence for A6.3 (recognising and writing 7), A6.5 (composing 7 in two ways), and indirectly A6.4 (subitizing parts of the split). Walk the room and check each child's two splits — score A6.5 on the spot.
3. Phase 2 (Decompose, 8 min): Children take a target group of 8 cubes and break it into two smaller groups, recording each break on a tens-frame mat. This produces visible evidence for A6.5 (decomposing 8) and A6.2 (counting forward/backward across the split). Begin one-on-one conferences during this phase — ask each child 'what two smaller groups did you make?' to confirm they can articulate the part-part-whole.
4. Phase 3 (Compare, 10 min): Pairs receive two plates of timbits (e.g., 6 and 9) and use the comparison strip to line them up and decide MORE / LESS / EQUAL. The child writes the answer on the worksheet. This produces visible evidence for A6.6 (comparing groups). Listen during the partner conversation for which child can articulate 'this plate has 3 MORE than that one' (Level 4 territory).
5. After the lesson: review the rubric with the worksheet artefact in front of you. Cross-reference with the formative-tracker rows (L2, L3, L4) for any child you are uncertain about — the Lesson 1 diagnostic plus three formative trackers plus the Lesson 5 evidence together cover all six expectations. For any child below Level 3 on any expectation, note what evidence (or absence) drove that decision. Use this for the Communication of Learning narrative and the morning-meeting small-group plan for next week.

**Scoring guidance:** Use the worksheet as primary evidence for A6.3, A6.5, and A6.6. Use the formative trackers (L2, L3, L4) as confirming evidence for A6.1, A6.2, and A6.4 — these expectations are best evidenced during their own lesson, not during the Lesson 5 summative. Level 3 is the grade-level goal — aim for most children to land at Level 2 or 3. Children who are Level 4 across all six expectations are ready for Grade 1 number sense work; document this for the receiving teacher. Children at Level 1 on multiple expectations need targeted small-group work; do not retain them in K number sense — instead build the bridge through concrete play (cube splits, comparison plates) and revisit during the next math block.

#### Evidence per expectation

- **A6.1** — Use the Lesson 1 diagnostic tracker as primary evidence — children who identified at least three numbers around the room and articulated the job of each are at Level 3 minimum. Confirm during Lesson 5 by asking 'where else did we see numbers this week?' during the closing circle.
- **A6.2** — Use the Lesson 2 formative tracker as primary evidence — children who narrated the count forward AND backward AND used 'bigger/smaller' correctly are at Level 3. Confirm during Lesson 5 Phase 2 by asking the child to count the cubes back into the basket and articulate 'the pile is getting smaller'.
- **A6.3** — Look at the worksheet's recorded splits in Phase 1 and the tens-frame in Phase 2. A child who writes the numerals 7, 4, 3, 5, 2, 8, 6, 2 cleanly (no reversals) across the worksheet is at Level 3 on A6.3. Cross-reference with the Lesson 3 formative tracker for teen-number evidence (the worksheet only requires writing 0–10).
- **A6.4** — Use the Lesson 4 formative tracker subitize-score column as primary evidence. A child with 4/5 or 5/5 is at Level 3. During Lesson 5 Phase 1, listen for whether the child subitizes the small parts of their cube split (e.g., looks at 4 cubes and says 'that's four' without counting one-by-one) — this is Level 4 evidence.
- **A6.5** — Look at Phase 1 of the worksheet — did the child build 7 in two genuinely different ways (not just rearranged in space)? Two splits with different colour counts (e.g., 5 red + 2 blue, AND 4 red + 3 blue) is Level 3. Three or more different splits is Level 4. Use Phase 2's tens-frame split as confirming evidence.
- **A6.6** — Look at Phase 3 of the worksheet — did the child correctly circle MORE, LESS, or EQUAL for the two timbit plates? Level 3 requires correctly lining up the plates on the comparison strip and choosing the right word. Level 4 requires articulating the size of the difference ('3 more').
